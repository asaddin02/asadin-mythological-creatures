
=== demon (lengkap-informasi, batch-033)

demon-c01 (traditional-belief): A demon is a supernatural entity usually regarded as evil or malevolent.
demon-c02 (etymology): Demon translates Greek daimon, meaning spirit, which could be good or evil.
demon-c03 (religious-tradition): In the Bible, demon denotes an evil spirit.
demon-c04 (traditional-belief): In ancient Mesopotamia, demons were not always evil; Pazuzu could also do good.
demon-c05 (scholarly-interpretation): The Met notes that ancient sources often portray these beings without a strict moral division.
demon-c06 (historical-record): Pazuzu was used for protection against Lamashtu, feared by pregnant women and newborns.
demon-c07 (etymology): Still later the Greek word δαιμόνιον (borrowed into Latin as daemonium) was applied to evil spirits that tormented men or caused them harm.
demon-c08 (scholarly-interpretation): The modern evil connotation of demon differs from its ancient usage.
demon-c09 (scholarly-interpretation): Demons may or may not be considered devils, minions of the Devil; in many traditions demons are independent operators, each causing different kinds of evils such as destructive natural phenomena or specific diseases.
demon-c10 (religious-tradition): In ancient Near Eastern and Abrahamic religions a demon is considered a harmful spiritual entity that may cause possession, calling for an exorcism.
demon-c11 (scholarly-interpretation): Demons are still feared largely because of their alleged power to possess living creatures.
demon-c12 (etymology): The term first acquired negative connotations in the Septuagint translation of the Hebrew Bible into Greek, which drew on the mythology of ancient Semitic religions.
demon-c13 (etymology): The English use of demon as a synonym for devils goes back at least as far as about 825.
demon-c14 (scholarly-interpretation): Belief in demons probably goes back to the Paleolithic age, stemming from humanity's fear of the unknown, the strange and the horrific.
demon-c15 (scholarly-interpretation): Ancient Egyptian demons can be divided into two classes: "guardians" and "wanderers".
demon-c16 (scholarly-interpretation): "Guardians" are tied to a specific place and their function can be benevolent toward those with the secret knowledge to face them.
demon-c17 (scholarly-interpretation): The "wanderers" are associated with possession, mental illness, death and plagues.
demon-c18 (scholarly-interpretation): The demons of Mesopotamia were generally hostile spirits of lesser power than a deity.
demon-c19 (traditional-belief): People could protect themselves from demons by wearing amulets, using magic, or seeking refuge with another demon or deity.
demon-c20 (traditional-belief): In the Babylonian Great Flood tale, since the gods promised never to flood humanity again, the demoness Lamaštu was installed instead, tasked with killing humans to prevent excessive multiplication.
demon-c21 (traditional-belief): One class of demons believed to dwell in the underworld were the galla, whose primary purpose appears to have been dragging unfortunate mortals back to Kur.
demon-c22 (traditional-belief): According to the Babylonian creation epic Enūma Eliš, both gods and demons are children of Tiamat, goddess of primordial chaos.
demon-c23 (religious-tradition): In the Younger Avesta demons (daēuua) cause diseases and death; ritual purification and recitation of sacred prayers can ward them off.
demon-c24 (religious-tradition): In medieval Christianity demons and spirits were generally considered fallen angels.
demon-c25 (religious-tradition): The Hebrew Bible mentions two classes of demonic spirits, the se'irim and the shedim.
demon-c26 (traditional-belief): Various diseases and ailments were ascribed to demons, particularly those affecting the brain and internal organs.
demon-c27 (traditional-belief): Demons are believed to be vanquished by sacred symbols, the content of which depends on the prevailing religion of the culture.
demon-c28 (traditional-belief): Common features of these Middle Eastern demons are immortality and pernicious nature; they can turn invisible and can be enslaved when pierced by a silver needle.
demon-c29 (traditional-belief): In some tales demons are given supernatural powers such as causing sickness, mental illness, or even turning humans to stone.
demon-c30 (scholarly-interpretation): In Islamic culture demons share many anthropomorphic attributes with the Arabian jinn and can enter sexual relationships with humans, but differ from jinn in being perceived as malign.
demon-c31 (traditional-belief): A ceremonial magician usually consults a grimoire, which gives the names and abilities of demons and detailed instructions for conjuring and controlling them.
demon-c32 (traditional-belief): In an Uighur Manichaean text fragment the demons quarrel over three magical items: a cap that makes people invisible, sandals that carry people anywhere, and a staff belonging to the sandals.
demon-c33 (scholarly-interpretation): According to Britannica 1911, only spirits believed to enter into relations with the human race are classified as demons.
demon-c34 (scholarly-interpretation): According to Britannica 1911, ascribing malevolence to the spirit world is by no means universal.
demon-c35 (scholarly-interpretation): According to Britannica 1911, disease-causing spirits may be human or non-human and may enter the victim's body.
demon-c36 (scholarly-interpretation): Britannica 1911 calls ancient Babylonian demonology especially complicated: petty annoyances such as a sudden fall, a headache, or a quarrel were set down to fiends.
demon-c37 (scholarly-interpretation): Britannica 1911 notes that, according to Hesiod, the men of the golden race became guardians or watchers over mortals after death.
demon-c38 (scholarly-interpretation): Britannica 1911 names incubi and succubi as a class of nocturnal demons said to consort with human beings in their sleep.
demon-c39 (scholarly-interpretation): According to Britannica 1911, the gods of an older religion sometimes degenerate into the demons of the belief that supersedes it.
demon-c40 (etymology): The Greek word δαίμων, from which English demon ultimately derives, originally meant a divine being normally regarded as good.
demon-c41 (etymology): Later this word and, more often, its neuter form δαιμόνιον were used of lesser spirits and demigods, especially guardian spirits or spirits that influenced men's characters.
demon-c42 (religious-tradition): In the late Old Testament books and rabbinical writings demons were regarded as causing physical harm and seducing people to moral evil, and so were considered God's enemies.
demon-c43 (religious-tradition): According to Jewish speculation, demons were fallen angels whose fall consisted either in having sexual intercourse with women or in rebelling against God under Satan's leadership.
demon-c44 (traditional-belief): In folk art demons were commonly portrayed with bat wings, horns, a pointed tail, and birdlike claws.
demon-c45 (historical-record): In the Middle Ages demons were portrayed in the most horrible forms artists could imagine, especially tormenting the damned in hell.
demon-c46 (historical-record): Representations of demons in Christian Western art began only in the 12th century but have been very common since.
