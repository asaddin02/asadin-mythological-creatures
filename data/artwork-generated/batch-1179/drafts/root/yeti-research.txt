
=== yeti (lengkap-informasi, batch-037)
Yeti | tier rich | cryptid | jenis kriptid | tradition-tibetan
Ringkas: The yeti is a hairy ape- or human-like creature in Himalayan and Tibetan lore, depicted in varied ways; tested purported yeti samples matched local bears.
yeti-c01 (traditional-belief): Written accounts of the Tibetan Yeti legend date to the twelfth century.
yeti-c02 (traditional-belief): Some accounts portray the yeti as a reddish-brown-haired, ape-like biped.
yeti-c03 (traditional-belief): Tibetan tradition has two-legged yetis and larger four-legged ones said to prey on livestock.
yeti-c04 (traditional-belief): In the stories, yetis are believed to inhabit alpine forests below the snow line.
yeti-c05 (traditional-belief): In many stories, the yeti is nocturnal and makes a high-pitched whistle or screech.
yeti-c06 (religious-tradition): Tibetan Buddhist stories feature both kind and malevolent yetis.
yeti-c07 (modern-popular-culture): The white-furred film depiction lacks folkloric support before the twentieth century.
yeti-c08 (scholarly-interpretation): Mitochondrial DNA analysis of purported yeti samples supported their identification as local bears.
yeti-c09 (scholarly-interpretation): Researchers associate the yeti legend with local bears while acknowledging uncertainty around the wider stories.
yeti-c10 (historical-record): Reports of purported yeti footprints spurred Western climbers' interest in the 1950s.
yeti-c11 (historical-record): Nepal issued rules for yeti-seeking expeditions.
yeti-c12 (traditional-belief): The yeti is a large, hairy, mythical humanoid creature said to inhabit the Himalayan mountain range in Asia.
yeti-c13 (traditional-belief): The yeti is often described as a large bipedal human- or ape-like creature covered with brown, grey or white hair, and sometimes with large sharp teeth.
yeti-c14 (scholarly-interpretation): Folklorists trace the origin of the yeti to a combination of factors, including Sherpa folklore and misidentified fauna such as bear or yak.
yeti-c15 (traditional-belief): Tibetan lore describes three main varieties of yeti: the black-furred nyalmo, the largest and fiercest (about fifteen feet tall); the chuti (about eight feet tall) living at 8,000-10,000 ft; and the reddish-brown rang shim bombo, only 3-5 ft tall.
yeti-c16 (historical-record): The name "abominable snowman" was coined in 1921, the year Lieutenant-Colonel Charles Howard-Bury led the British Mount Everest reconnaissance expedition.
yeti-c17 (historical-record): The term "abominable snowman" began when Henry Newman interviewed the expedition's porters on their return to Darjeeling and mistranslated the word "metoh" as "filthy".
yeti-c18 (etymology): According to Wikipedia, "metoh" translates as "man-bear" and "kang-mi" as "snowman".
yeti-c19 (scholarly-interpretation): According to H. Siiger, the yeti was part of the pre-Buddhist beliefs of several Himalayan peoples.
yeti-c20 (traditional-belief): Followers of the Bön religion once believed the blood of the "Mi rgod" or "wild man" was used in certain spiritual ceremonies; the being was depicted as ape-like, carrying a large stone as a weapon and making a whistling swoosh.
yeti-c21 (religious-tradition): The yeti was adopted into Tibetan Buddhism as a nonhuman animal human enough to sometimes follow Dharma; several stories feature yetis as helpers and disciples to religious figures.
yeti-c22 (religious-tradition): In Tibet, images of yetis are paraded and occasionally worshipped as guardians against evil spirits.
yeti-c23 (religious-tradition): Because yetis sometimes act as enforcers of Dharma, hearing or seeing one is often considered a bad omen for which the witness must accumulate merit.
yeti-c24 (historical-record): In 1832 the Journal of the Asiatic Society of Bengal published B. H. Hodgson's account of northern Nepal: his guides spotted a tall bipedal creature with long dark hair that seemed to flee, which Hodgson concluded was an orangutan.
yeti-c25 (historical-record): In 1951, while attempting to scale Mount Everest, Eric Shipton photographed a number of large prints in the snow at about 6,000 m.
yeti-c26 (historical-record): In 1953, Sir Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay reported seeing large footprints while scaling Everest; Hillary later discounted yeti reports as unreliable.
yeti-c27 (scholarly-interpretation): In 1986, mountaineer Reinhold Messner claimed in his autobiography My Quest for the Yeti that the yeti is actually the endangered Himalayan brown bear.
yeti-c28 (scholarly-interpretation): Misidentification of Himalayan wildlife has been proposed to explain some sightings, including the chu-teh (a lower-altitude langur monkey), the Tibetan blue bear, or the Himalayan brown bear (dzu-teh).
yeti-c29 (historical-record): The yeti is the mysterious humanoid creature reported by Western sources as early as 1832 as living in the Himalayas, and it became well known following several expeditions in the 1950s.
yeti-c30 (historical-record): In 1960 Sir Edmund Hillary, who conquered Everest, called further attention to the yeti through his attempts to debunk them.
yeti-c31 (historical-record): According to a TASS report of January 9, 1988, the Soviet Ministry of Culture established a group of "cryptozoologists" to locate the yeti.
yeti-c32 (etymology): The term is recorded in English from the 1930s and comes from Tibetan yeh-teh, "little manlike animal".
yeti-c33 (etymology): Yeti is the native Sherpa (Tibetan) name of the Abominable Snowman, a subhuman animal supposed to leave tracks in the snow of the Himalayas.
yeti-c34 (historical-record): The Oxford dictionary describes the yeti as a large hairy creature resembling a human or bear, said to live in the highest part of the Himalayas.
yeti-c35 (etymology): "Bun manchi" is Nepali for "jungle man", used outside Sherpa communities where "yeti" is the common name.
yeti-c36 (traditional-belief): Local legend holds that "anyone who sees one dies or is killed".
yeti-c37 (scholarly-interpretation): According to Wikipedia, Pranavananda states that the words "ti", "te" and "teh" derive from the spoken word "tre" (spelled "dred"), Tibetan for bear.
