
=== gaia (lengkap-informasi, batch-038)
Gaia | tier rich | deity | jenis dewa | greek-mythology
Ringkas: Gaia is the primordial Earth goddess of Greek myth, mother of gods, Titans and Giants, adviser to Zeus, and in one tradition the original owner of the Delphic oracle.
Kemampuan: null — Gaia bore Heaven, the mountains, and Pontus, then Titans, Cyclopes, Hecatoncheires, Erinyes and Giants; Theoi calls her the great mother of all creation. [gaia-c04, gaia-c13, gaia-c31, gaia-c16, gaia-c25]
Kemampuan: prophecy — Gaia was regarded as an oracular goddess; she foretold that Cronus would be overthrown by his son and that Zeus would have a strong child, and Cronos learned his fate from her and Heaven. [gaia-c29, gaia-c34, gaia-c17]
Kemampuan: null — Gaia made a great sickle of grey flint, told her plan to her sons, and hid Cronos in an ambush. [gaia-c14, gaia-c15]

gaia-c01 (traditional-belief): Gaia is the primordial personification of Earth in Greek myth.
gaia-c02 (traditional-belief): In one creation account Gaia emerges from Chaos and is regarded as a mother goddess.
gaia-c03 (scholarly-interpretation): Gaia's Roman equivalent is Terra Mater or Tellus Mater.
gaia-c04 (traditional-belief): According to Hesiod, Gaia bore Uranus, Ourea, and Pontus.
gaia-c05 (traditional-belief): Gaia and Uranus parented the twelve Titans.
gaia-c06 (traditional-belief): Gaia opposed Uranus after he hid their children deep in Earth.
gaia-c07 (traditional-belief): Gaia advised Zeus to free the Hecatoncheires and Cyclopes during the Titan war.
gaia-c08 (traditional-belief): After the Titans' defeat, Gaia sent the Giants against the Olympian gods.
gaia-c09 (traditional-belief): Gaia was said to bear Typhon with Tartarus.
gaia-c10 (traditional-belief): One tradition holds that the Delphic oracle originally belonged to Gaia.
gaia-c11 (historical-record): Greek vase painting shows Gaia as a matronly woman rising from Earth.
gaia-c12 (traditional-belief): In Hesiod's Theogony, Chaos came first, and next wide-bosomed Earth.
gaia-c13 (traditional-belief): According to Hesiod, Earth first bore starry Heaven, equal to herself, to cover her on every side and be an abiding place for the blessed gods.
gaia-c14 (traditional-belief): In Hesiod, a straitened Earth groaned, made a great sickle from grey flint, and told her plan to her sons.
gaia-c15 (traditional-belief): In Hesiod, the wily Cronos took courage and answered his mother, and Earth rejoiced, hid him in an ambush, put a jagged sickle in his hands, and revealed the whole plot to him.
gaia-c16 (traditional-belief): According to Hesiod, Earth received the drops of blood and later bore the strong Erinyes and the great Giants with gleaming armour.
gaia-c17 (traditional-belief): According to Hesiod, Cronos swallowed his children as each was born because he learned from Earth and starry Heaven that he was destined to be overcome by his own son.
gaia-c18 (traditional-belief): According to Hesiod, Earth carried Zeus through the black night and hid him in a remote cave on Mount Aegeum.
gaia-c19 (traditional-belief): According to Hesiod, Earth gave a great stone wrapped in swaddling clothes to the mightily ruling son of Heaven, the earlier king of the gods.
gaia-c20 (traditional-belief): According to Hesiod, the wily Cronos was beguiled by Earth's deep suggestions and brought up his offspring again.
gaia-c21 (traditional-belief): According to Hesiod, the gods brought them up again to the light at Earth's advising; she explained that with them the gods would gain victory.
gaia-c22 (traditional-belief): According to Hesiod, Earth bore her youngest child Typhoeus from the love of Tartarus.
gaia-c23 (traditional-belief): According to Hesiod, three other sons were born of Earth and Heaven, great and doughty beyond telling: Cottus, Briareos and Gyes.
gaia-c24 (scholarly-interpretation): Gaia was the goddess of the earth.
gaia-c25 (scholarly-interpretation): According to Theoi, Gaia was the great mother of all creation: the heavenly gods came from her union with Ouranos, the sea-gods from Pontos, the Giants from Tartaros, and mortal creatures directly from her flesh.
gaia-c26 (scholarly-interpretation): According to Theoi, Gaia was the chief antagonist of the heavenly gods; first she rebelled against her husband Ouranos, who had imprisoned several of her giant-sons within her womb.
gaia-c27 (scholarly-interpretation): According to Theoi, Gaia birthed a tribe of Giants and later the monster Typhoeus to overthrow Zeus, but both failed.
gaia-c28 (historical-record): Gaia appears as a divine being as early as the Homeric poems; in the Iliad black sheep were sacrificed to her and she was invoked in oaths.
gaia-c29 (scholarly-interpretation): Because Ge was seen as the source of the vapours producing divine inspiration, she herself was also regarded as an oracular divinity.
gaia-c30 (historical-record): Her worship appears to have been universal among the Greeks, with temples or altars at Athens, Sparta, Delphi, Olympia, Bura, Tegea, Phlyus and other places.
gaia-c31 (traditional-belief): Gaia also bore the three one-eyed Cyclopes and the three Hecatoncheires, the hundred-handed and fifty-headed Giants.
gaia-c32 (traditional-belief): With Pontus, Gaia gave birth to the sea gods and goddesses Thaumas, Phorcys, Ceto, and Eurybia.
gaia-c33 (traditional-belief): A pregnant Rhea approached Gaia for help, and Gaia helped her escape to Crete, where she gave birth to Zeus.
gaia-c34 (traditional-belief): Gaia foretold that Cronus would be overthrown by his son and prophesied that Zeus and Metis would have a strong, wise daughter and a son who would become king of the gods.
gaia-c35 (traditional-belief): An oracle said the gods needed a mortal made invulnerable by a magic herb to defeat the Giants; Gaia searched for the herb, but Zeus prevented her from finding it.
gaia-c36 (traditional-belief): The Giants were unmatched in size and strength and fearful to behold, with feet of dragon scales and long hair.
gaia-c37 (traditional-belief): Gaia gave Zeus and Hera golden apples from a magical tree that granted immortality to anyone who possessed them.
gaia-c38 (traditional-belief): According to some writers, Gaia transformed Daphne into a laurel tree to save her when she cried out for help.
gaia-c39 (traditional-belief): Other sources say Apollo stole the oracle from Gaia after killing Python, the serpent who guarded the Delphic Oracle.
gaia-c40 (traditional-belief): According to the ancient poem Eumolpia, Gaia and Poseidon shared the oracle until Gaia gave her share to Themis.
gaia-c41 (traditional-belief): After giving up Delphi, Gaia was still consulted as an oracle at Aegeira in Achaea, where her priestess drank bull's blood.
gaia-c42 (religious-tradition): Gaia has an Orphic Hymn dedicated to her, calling her the "mother of men" and the nourisher and giver of all.
gaia-c43 (traditional-belief): According to Orphism, Phanes gave birth to Nyx and then had Gaia and Uranus with her.
gaia-c44 (historical-record): According to Hesiod (c. 700 BCE) in his Theogony, Gaia emerged from Chaos.
gaia-c45 (traditional-belief): In revenge, Gaia lay with Tartarus and bore her youngest child, Typhon, the biggest monster ever born.
