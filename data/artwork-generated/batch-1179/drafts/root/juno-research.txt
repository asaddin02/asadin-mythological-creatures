
=== juno (lengkap-informasi, batch-036)
Juno | tier rich | deity | jenis dewa | tradition-roman
Ringkas: Juno is a Roman goddess who protected the state and every aspect of women's lives, wife and sister of Jupiter, known by epithets such as Regina, Lucina, Sospita and Moneta.

juno-c01 (religious-tradition): Juno is a Roman goddess who protected the nation and women's lives.
juno-c02 (scholarly-interpretation): Juno is often equated with the Greek goddess Hera.
juno-c03 (religious-tradition): Juno was Jupiter's wife and sister and was worshipped with Minerva.
juno-c04 (historical-record): Ancient authors associate Juno Regina with Veii; Regina means queen.
juno-c05 (religious-tradition): As Juno Lucina she was a goddess of childbirth.
juno-c06 (religious-tradition): Juno protected legally married women.
juno-c07 (historical-record): Juno's Matronalia festival fell on March 1.
juno-c08 (historical-record): A peacock identifies Juno in art.
juno-c09 (traditional-belief): In Aeneas's story, Juno loved Carthage, which was prophesied to be destroyed by Rome.
juno-c10 (religious-tradition): As Juno Sospita she was the chief deity of Lanuvium, a city in Latium southeast of Rome; she protected those in confinement and was often pictured wearing a goatskin and carrying a spear and shield.
juno-c11 (religious-tradition): As Juno Moneta she is called the moon goddess, a personage unique to Rome.
juno-c12 (historical-record): Her primary temple was built on the Arx, the northern part of Capitoline Hill, next to the Roman mint; the word "money" comes from her name, Moneta.
juno-c13 (religious-tradition): Her sacred geese were kept on the Capitoline, and legend says they warned the Roman military under Manlius Capitolinus when the invading Gauls tried to take Rome in 390 BCE.
juno-c14 (religious-tradition): At her temple no offering could be made unless all knots were untied, because a belt could hinder the delivery of the woman's child.
juno-c15 (religious-tradition): On the Matronalia husbands were expected to give presents to their wives; the day was supposedly celebrating the birthday of her son Mars, the god of war.
juno-c16 (religious-tradition): According to legend Juno was connected with the city of Carthage, where she was identified with the goddess Tanit.
juno-c17 (religious-tradition): In the Aeneas story a storm at sea created by Juno failed when Neptune interfered, not out of love for Aeneas but out of irritation at Juno meddling in his domain.
juno-c18 (historical-record): Her exact origin is unknown, but Juno was one of the oldest Roman deities, one of the three original gods (Jupiter, Juno, Minerva) honored on the Quirinal and later on the Capitoline.
juno-c19 (religious-tradition): Under whatever name she was known, Juno presided over every aspect of a woman's life.
juno-c20 (religious-tradition): Juno was the chief Roman and Latin goddess and the special object of worship by women at all the critical moments of life.
juno-c21 (scholarly-interpretation): Juno was especially a deity of women and in a sense represents the female principle of life, as shown by the fact that every man had his genius and every woman her Juno.
juno-c22 (religious-tradition): As Caprotina she was worshipped especially by female slaves on 7 July (Nonae Caprotinae); as Sospita she was invoked all over Latium as the saviour of women in peril and later of the state.
juno-c23 (historical-record): In historical times she was associated with Jupiter in the great temple on the Capitoline hill as Juno Regina, queen of all the Junones or queen of heaven.
juno-c24 (etymology): The etymology of the name is uncertain, but it is usually taken as a shortened form of Jovino, answering to Jovis, from a root div meaning shining.
juno-c25 (scholarly-interpretation): According to this source it was again under Greek influence that she became Jupiter's wife and Mars's mother; the true Roman had no such personal interest in his deities as to invent family relations for them.
juno-c26 (scholarly-interpretation): Under Greek influence Juno was early identified with Hera, with whom she shares much cult and character; thus the Juno familiar from Latin literature is not the true Roman deity.
juno-c27 (religious-tradition): Juno is the daughter of Saturn and Ops, sister and wife of Jupiter, and mother of Mars, Vulcan, Bellona, Lucina and Juventas; like Hera her sacred animal is the peafowl, and her Etruscan counterpart is Uni.
juno-c28 (religious-tradition): Her warlike aspect among the Romans is apparent in her attire; she was often shown armed and wearing a goatskin cloak.
juno-c29 (religious-tradition): As patron goddess of Rome and the Roman Empire Juno was called Regina ("Queen") and belongs to the Capitoline Triad (Juno Capitolina) with Jupiter and Minerva, goddess of wisdom.
juno-c30 (scholarly-interpretation): The view that she was also a moon goddess is no longer accepted by scholars, as that role belongs to Diana Lucifera.
juno-c31 (etymology): A derivation was later proposed from iuven- (as in iuvenis, "youth") through a syncopated form iūn-.
juno-c32 (scholarly-interpretation): Juno is certainly the divine protectress of the community, showing both a sovereign and a fertility character, often associated with a military one.
juno-c33 (scholarly-interpretation): The epithet Lucina reflects two interrelated aspects of Juno's function: the cyclical renewal of time in the waning and waxing moon, and protection of delivery and birth.
juno-c34 (religious-tradition): Juno is closely associated with Janus, the god of passages and beginnings, who is often named Iunonius after her.
juno-c35 (religious-tradition): According to Virgil, there was little love between Aeneas, the Trojan hero of the story, and the goddess.
juno-c36 (scholarly-interpretation): Virgil used Juno as the story's antagonist, although the name of Hera could just as easily be substituted.
juno-c37 (historical-record): She received a temple on the Aventine Hill and served as a civic deity, the protector of the state.
juno-c38 (historical-record): On 1 March, the old Roman New Year's day, the matrons met and made offerings at her temple in a grove on the Esquiline, hence the name Matronalia.
juno-c39 (scholarly-interpretation): Some authors claim the festival actually celebrated the anniversary of the end of the Roman-Sabine War and honored the role women played.
juno-c40 (religious-tradition): Juno was the wife and sister of Jupiter, and the two were worshipped along with Minerva on the Quirinal in Rome.
juno-c41 (historical-record): Under the same title she was enticed from Veii after its capture in 392 B.C. and settled in a temple on the Aventine.
