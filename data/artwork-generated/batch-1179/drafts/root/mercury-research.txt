
=== mercury (lengkap-informasi, batch-036)
Mercury | tier rich | deity | jenis dewa | tradition-roman

mercury-c01 (religious-tradition): Mercury is the Roman god of commerce and mediator between gods and mortals.
mercury-c02 (religious-tradition): Mercury protected merchants and travelers.
mercury-c03 (traditional-belief): Mercury was said to be the son of Jupiter and Maia.
mercury-c04 (etymology): Mercury is associated with Hermes; Mercurius may derive from merx.
mercury-c05 (religious-tradition): Mercury escorted the dead to the underworld.
mercury-c06 (historical-record): Mercury is depicted with a caduceus and winged sandals.
mercury-c07 (historical-record): Mercury's festival day was May 15.
mercury-c08 (traditional-belief): Mercury reminded Aeneas to leave Dido and Carthage.
mercury-c09 (historical-record): Roman statuettes show Mercury holding a staff and purse.
mercury-c10 (religious-tradition): Allegedly, while escorting the nymph Lara to Pluto's realm, he fathered the twin Lares, guardian gods of Rome.
mercury-c11 (historical-record): A temple honoring him was built around 495 BCE on the southwest slope of the Aventine Hill near the Circus Maximus of Rome.
mercury-c12 (historical-record): A cult honoring him existed outside the city of Rome in Campania and Latium, as well as in Gaul and Britain.
mercury-c13 (religious-tradition): Besides the wand and sandals, he wore a broad-brimmed hat, the Petasus, and carried a purse, a symbol of his duties as god of commerce and profits.
mercury-c14 (religious-tradition): To many he was also known as cunning and shrewd as well as a deceiver, often pulling pranks on the unsuspecting, especially the god Apollo.
mercury-c15 (religious-tradition): Besides being the god of commerce, Mercury was patron of shepherds, cheats and thieves; Ovid relays a story of Mercury as a master thief himself.
mercury-c16 (religious-tradition): In Ovid's story of Battus, after being promised a heifer and a bull, the old man revealed everything; an angry Mercury turned him into stone.
mercury-c17 (religious-tradition): To free Io, Mercury told Argus boring stories until he fell asleep; after Mercury beheaded Argus, Io was freed.
mercury-c18 (religious-tradition): In Roman mythology it is Mercury who escorts the young Proserpina, daughter of Ceres, to the underworld to be with Pluto (the Greek Hades).
mercury-c19 (religious-tradition): One story shows Mercury as a small child, though it is more often attributed to Hermes: he built a lyre from a tortoiseshell, then one night slipped away from his mother and stole Apollo's oxen.
mercury-c20 (religious-tradition): In Greek mythology the wand had been given to him by Apollo.
mercury-c21 (scholarly-interpretation): As with many Roman myths, narratives about Mercury became intertwined with those of Hermes, so it is often difficult to separate the two.
mercury-c22 (religious-tradition): Merchants would pray to him for high profits and protection of their trade goods.
mercury-c23 (historical-record): In 495 the Greek god Hermes was introduced into Rome under the Italian name Mercurius, as protector of the grain trade, especially with Sicily.
mercury-c24 (religious-tradition): On the 15th of May every shopman drew water from this spring and sprinkled it with a laurel twig over his head and goods, entreating Mercury to remove the guilt of all his deceits.
mercury-c25 (etymology): The word mercurialis was popularly used as equivalent to "cheat".
mercury-c26 (scholarly-interpretation): The 15th of May was chosen as Mercury's feast because Maia was the mother of Hermes, that is, of Mercury; on this day she was worshipped with her son by the mercuriales.
mercury-c27 (religious-tradition): Mercury (Mercurius), in Roman mythology, was the god of merchandise (merx) and merchants, later identified with the Greek Hermes.
mercury-c28 (religious-tradition): He is the god of boundaries, commerce, communication (including divination), eloquence, financial gain, languages, luck, thieves, travelers and trickery, and the guide of souls to the underworld.
mercury-c29 (scholarly-interpretation): In Roman mythology he was the son of Maia, daughter of the Titan Atlas, and Jupiter; in his earliest forms he appears related to the Etruscan deity Turms.
mercury-c30 (religious-tradition): He was often accompanied by a rooster, herald of the new day, a ram or goat symbolizing fertility, and a tortoise referring to his legendary invention of the lyre from a tortoise shell.
mercury-c31 (scholarly-interpretation): Mercury was not among the di indigetes of early Roman religion; he subsumed the earlier Dei Lucrii as Roman religion was syncretized with Greek religion under the Republic, starting around the 4th century BC.
mercury-c32 (historical-record): Julius Caesar wrote that Mercury was the most popular god in Britain and Gaul, regarded as inventor of all the arts; in Roman syncretism he was equated with the Celtic god Lugus and commonly accompanied by the goddess Rosmerta.
mercury-c33 (scholarly-interpretation): The Romans associated Mercury with the Germanic god Wotan; Tacitus identifies him as the chief god of the Germanic peoples, and the link is seen in the day-names Wednesday and Mercredi.
mercury-c34 (religious-tradition): There was a spring dedicated to Mercury between his temple and the Porta Capena; every shopman drew water from it on the 15th of May.
mercury-c35 (historical-record): Archaeological evidence from Pompeii suggests that Mercury was among the most popular Roman gods.
mercury-c36 (modern-popular-culture): Mercury features in Jack Kirby's first published comic book story, "Mercury in the 20th Century", in Red Raven Comics 1, 1940.
mercury-c37 (historical-record): Mercury's temple in Rome stood in the Circus Maximus, between the Aventine and Palatine Hills, and was built in 495 BC.
mercury-c38 (historical-record): Mercury became the god not only of the mercatores and the grain trade but of buying and selling in general; in streets with many shops, little chapels and images of the god appear to have been erected.
mercury-c39 (etymology): The name Mercury is possibly related to the Latin words merx ("merchandise"), mercari (to trade) and merces (wages).
mercury-c40 (religious-tradition): Like his Greek equivalent Hermes, he was given a magic wand by Apollo, which later became the caduceus, the staff with intertwined snakes.
mercury-c41 (religious-tradition): Ovid wrote that Mercury carried Morpheus's dreams from the valley of Somnus to sleeping humans.
mercury-c42 (historical-record): He is known to the Romans as Mercurius and occasionally in earlier writings as Merqurius, Mirqurios or Mircurios.
mercury-c43 (historical-record): Mercury was also considered a god of abundance and commercial success, particularly in Gaul, where he was said to be especially revered.
