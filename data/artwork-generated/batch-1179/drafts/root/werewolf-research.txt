werewolf-c39 (traditional-belief): In an Armenian story cited by Baring-Gould, a man found a wolf-skin in a cave and cast it into a fire; a woman appeared, howled and tried to rescue it, and when the hide was consumed she vanished in the smoke.
werewolf-c40 (traditional-belief): Among the White Russians, a man who has incurred the devil's wrath is punished by being turned into a wolf and sent among his relations, who recognize and feed him.

werewolf-c42 (traditional-belief): The Poles have werewolves that rage twice a year, at Christmas and at midsummer.
werewolf-c43 (etymology): In folklore a werewolf (from Old English werwulf 'man-wolf'), or occasionally lycanthrope (from Ancient Greek λυκάνθρωπος 'wolf-human'), is an individual who can shapeshift into a wolf or a wolf-humanlike hybrid, voluntarily or involuntarily due to a curse or other affliction.
werewolf-c44 (historical-record): According to Wikipedia, early sources for belief in this ability or affliction, called lycanthropy, are Petronius (27–66) and Gervase of Tilbury (1150–1228).
werewolf-c45 (historical-record): Belief in werewolves developed in parallel with belief in witches during the late Middle Ages and the early modern period.
werewolf-c46 (historical-record): Trials of supposed werewolves emerged in what is now Switzerland, especially the Valais and Vaud, in the early 15th century, spread across Europe in the 16th, peaked in the 17th and subsided by the 18th century.
werewolf-c47 (historical-record): The case of Peter Stumpp (1589) led to a significant peak of interest in and persecution of supposed werewolves, primarily in French- and German-speaking Europe.
werewolf-c48 (historical-record): The 16th-century Swedish writer Olaus Magnus says Livonian werewolves were initiated by draining a cup of specially prepared beer and repeating a set formula.
werewolf-c49 (etymology): Wikipedia records the Old French expression leus warous ~ lous garous (modern loup-garou), which literally means 'wolf-werewolf'.
werewolf-c50 (etymology): The Proto-Slavic noun *vьlko-dlakь, meaning 'wolf-haired', can be reconstructed from Serbian, Slovenian, and Czech forms, including Czech vlkodlak.
werewolf-c51 (etymology): An alternative reconstruction, *wazi-wulfaz ('wolf-clothed'), would bring the Germanic compound closer to the Slavic meaning.
werewolf-c52 (modern-popular-culture): Most modern fiction describes werewolves as vulnerable to silver weapons and highly resistant to other injuries; this feature appears in 19th-century German literature and tales, including those collected in folklore compilations.
werewolf-c53 (modern-popular-culture): The full moon as the cause of transformation only became part of the depiction of werewolves on a widespread basis in the twentieth century.
werewolf-c54 (modern-popular-culture): The first movie to feature the transformative effect of the full moon was Frankenstein Meets the Wolf Man in 1943.
werewolf-c55 (modern-popular-culture): The first feature film to use an anthropomorphic werewolf was Werewolf of London in 1935.
