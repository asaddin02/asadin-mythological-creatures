
=== phoenix (lengkap-informasi, batch-034)
Phoenix | tier rich | bird | jenis hewan mitos | greek-mythology
Ringkas: The phoenix is a legendary red-and-gold bird of Greek mythology that renews itself or is reborn from its predecessor, and is associated with the Sun.
Kemampuan: regeneration — According to Ovid the phoenix renews and re-begets itself; a new bird is reborn from its parent's body. [phoenix-c11, phoenix-c06, phoenix-c03]
Kemampuan: flight — In Herodotus's account the phoenix flies from Arabia to the temple of the Sun; in Ovid it carries the nest across the sky. [phoenix-c05, phoenix-c13]
Kemampuan: null — According to Aelian, the phoenix can reckon five hundred years without arithmetic. [phoenix-c18]
phoenix-c01 (traditional-belief): The phoenix is a legendary red-and-gold bird that radiates sunlight.
phoenix-c02 (traditional-belief): One account gives the phoenix a five-hundred-year life and a diet of Arabian balsam and frankincense.
phoenix-c03 (traditional-belief): When a phoenix dies, a new one emerges from its body.
phoenix-c04 (historical-record): Herodotus said he had never seen a phoenix, only pictures of it.
phoenix-c05 (traditional-belief): In Herodotus's account, the phoenix carries its parent's body from Arabia to a sun temple wrapped in myrrh.
phoenix-c06 (traditional-belief): Ovid says a young phoenix is reborn from its parent's body.
phoenix-c07 (scholarly-interpretation): According to a story that has gained more currency in modern times, the phoenix, on reaching a very old age (some say 500 and others 1461 years), committed itself to the flames.
phoenix-c08 (scholarly-interpretation): Chinese fenghuang is often translated 'phoenix' but is a distinct mythical bird.
phoenix-c09 (traditional-belief): The fenghuang is said to appear in times of peace and prosperity.
phoenix-c10 (historical-record): According to Herodotus, if the picture is accurate, the phoenix has partly golden and partly red plumage and is most like an eagle in shape and size.
phoenix-c11 (historical-record): According to Ovid, one bird alone renews and re-begets itself, the Phoenix of Assyria, which feeds on balsam oil and the tears of frankincense.
phoenix-c12 (historical-record): In Ovid's account the phoenix builds a nest high on a palm, lines it with cassia, spikenard, myrrh and cinnamon, and ends its life there.
phoenix-c13 (historical-record): In Ovid's account the young phoenix carries the nest across the sky to the Sun's city (Heliopolis in Egypt) and lays it before the doors of the Sun's temple.
phoenix-c14 (historical-record): In Philostratus the sage Iarchas says the phoenix gives out rays of sunlight, shines with gold, resembles an eagle, and sits on a spice nest it makes at the springs of the Nile.
phoenix-c15 (historical-record): In the Indian version reported by Philostratus, the phoenix being consumed in its nest sings funeral strains for itself.
phoenix-c16 (historical-record): According to Claudian, the phoenix needs no food or drink; the sun's beam is its food and the sea's spray its drink.
phoenix-c17 (historical-record): According to Claudian, this bird was never conceived and springs from no mortal seed; it is both its own father and son.
phoenix-c18 (historical-record): According to Aelian, the phoenix can reckon five hundred years without arithmetic, being a pupil of all-wise nature.
phoenix-c19 (historical-record): In one account the phoenix builds a nest in Arabia and gives it the power of generation, so that a new phoenix rises from it after the bird's death.
phoenix-c20 (historical-record): Another version says only one phoenix lived at a time, and when it died a worm crept from its body and was developed into a new phoenix by the sun's heat.
phoenix-c21 (historical-record): Another version says the phoenix built a pyre of spices, settled on it and died, then rose again from the decomposing body.
phoenix-c22 (scholarly-interpretation): Similar stories of marvellous birds occur in the East, such as the bird Simorg in Persia and the bird Semendar in India.
phoenix-c23 (historical-record): The new phoenix encases its parent in an egg of myrrh and conveys it to the great Egyptian temple of the Sun-God at Heliopolis.
phoenix-c24 (scholarly-interpretation): The phoenix is a legendary immortal bird that cyclically regenerates or is otherwise born again; it originates in Greek mythology and has analogues in many cultures.
phoenix-c25 (scholarly-interpretation): The phoenix is associated with the Sun and obtains new life by rising from the ashes of its predecessor.
phoenix-c26 (etymology): The Latin word comes from Greek φοῖνιξ (phoinix), first attested in Mycenaean Greek as po-ni-ke, which probably meant "griffin" though it might have meant "palm tree".
phoenix-c27 (etymology): The Greek word is probably a borrowing from a West Semitic word for madder, a red dye made from Rubia tinctorum.
phoenix-c28 (historical-record): The earliest clear mention of the phoenix in ancient Greek literature occurs in a fragment of the Precepts of Chiron, attributed to the 8th-century BC poet Hesiod.
phoenix-c29 (historical-record): In that fragment the phoenix outlives nine ravens, but the Nymphs, daughters of Zeus, outlive ten phoenixes.
phoenix-c30 (scholarly-interpretation): In the 19th century it was found that Egyptians at Heliopolis had venerated the Bennu, a solar bird similar in some respects to the Greek phoenix, though the Egyptian sources are often problematic and open to varied interpretations.
phoenix-c31 (scholarly-interpretation): The phoenix is often shown with a halo in ancient and medieval literature and medieval art, emphasising its connection with the Sun.
phoenix-c32 (scholarly-interpretation): The phoenix was generally believed to be colourful and vibrant, but sources give no clear consensus about its exact colouration.
phoenix-c33 (historical-record): Pliny states that a purported phoenix seen in Egypt in 47 AD was brought to the capital and exhibited in the Comitium.
phoenix-c34 (historical-record): Pliny added that nobody would doubt that this phoenix was a fabrication.
phoenix-c35 (historical-record): Tacitus recorded that the phoenix appeared in 34 AD.
phoenix-c36 (scholarly-interpretation): Scholars note analogues of the phoenix in many cultures, including the Hindu garuda and bherunda, the Slavic firebird and Raróg, and the Persian simorgh.
phoenix-c37 (modern-popular-culture): The Harry Potter series features a phoenix named Fawkes, Professor Dumbledore's loyal pet, and a secret society called the Order of the Phoenix.
