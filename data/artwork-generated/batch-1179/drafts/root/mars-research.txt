
=== mars (lengkap-informasi, batch-034)
Mars | tier rich | deity | jenis dewa | tradition-roman
Ringkas: Mars is the Roman god of war, second only to Jupiter, and a guardian of Rome and agriculture. He is known as father of Romulus and Remus, and the wolf and woodpecker were sacred to him.
Kemampuan: null — Mars is called upon to watch over the Roman people in war and to protect suppliants from ill. [mars-c28, mars-c24]
Kemampuan: null — As agricultural guardian, Mars directs his energies toward making crops grow, which may include warding off hostile forces of nature. [mars-c23]
Kemampuan: null — The appearance of the wolf of Mars at the Battle of Sentinum was a sign Roman victory was to come. [mars-c19]
mars-c01 (religious-tradition): Mars is the Roman war god, second only to Jupiter in the Roman pantheon.
mars-c02 (scholarly-interpretation): Mars myths drew on Greek Ares but retained distinct Roman features.
mars-c03 (religious-tradition): Mars was seen as protector of Rome and its borders.
mars-c04 (traditional-belief): Mars was said to father Romulus and Remus, Rome's mythical founders.
mars-c05 (historical-record): The month Martius was named after Mars.
mars-c06 (scholarly-interpretation): Some Mars rites may have agricultural links, but his agricultural role is debated.
mars-c07 (religious-tradition): The wolf and woodpecker were associated with Mars.
mars-c08 (historical-record): Temples and altars to Mars stood in Rome and across the empire.
mars-c09 (historical-record): A Roman relief at The Met portrays Mars as an older bearded man wearing a Corinthian helmet.
mars-c10 (historical-record): Mars festivals in March and October marked military campaign preparations and closure.
mars-c11 (traditional-belief): Mars is the god of war and also an agricultural guardian, a combination characteristic of early Rome; he is the son of Jupiter and Juno and was pre-eminent among the Roman army's military gods.
mars-c12 (scholarly-interpretation): Unlike Ares, who was seen mainly as a destructive and destabilizing force, Mars represented military power as a way to secure peace and was a father (pater) of the Roman people.
mars-c13 (historical-record): Mars's altar in the Campus Martius, the area of Rome named after him, was supposed to have been dedicated by Numa, the peace-loving semi-legendary second king of Rome.
mars-c14 (historical-record): Augustus shifted the focus of Mars's cult inside the pomerium (Rome's ritual boundary) and built a temple to Mars Ultor as a key religious feature of his new forum.
mars-c15 (traditional-belief): Mars's association with the wolf is familiar from what may be the most famous Roman myth: a she-wolf (lupa) suckled his infant sons when they were exposed on the order of King Amulius.
mars-c16 (traditional-belief): The wild animals most sacred to Mars were the woodpecker and the wolf, said in Roman natural lore always to inhabit the same foothills and woodlands.
mars-c17 (traditional-belief): Because the beak of the picus Martius held the god's power to ward off harm, it was carried as a magic charm to prevent bee stings and leech bites.
mars-c18 (traditional-belief): In the territory of the Aequi, Mars had an oracle of great antiquity where prophecies were supposed to be spoken by a woodpecker perched on a wooden column.
mars-c19 (historical-record): At the Battle of Sentinum in 295 BCE, the appearance of the wolf of Mars (Martius lupus) was a sign that Roman victory was to come.
mars-c20 (historical-record): In Roman Gaul the goose was associated with the Celtic forms of Mars, and archaeologists have found geese buried alongside warriors in graves.
mars-c21 (historical-record): The two most distinctive animal sacrifices to Mars were the suovetaurilia, a triple offering of pig, ram and bull, and the October Horse, the only horse sacrifice known in ancient Rome.
mars-c22 (historical-record): Cato the Elder invokes Mars Silvanus for a ritual to be carried out in silva, in the woods.
mars-c23 (scholarly-interpretation): As an agricultural guardian, Mars directs his energies toward creating conditions for crops to grow, which may include warding off hostile forces of nature.
mars-c24 (historical-record): Mars is invoked by the Arval Brotherhood specifically to protect and defend the suppliants from ill.
mars-c25 (scholarly-interpretation): Mars may have originated as a god of the wild who dwelt beyond the boundaries set by humans.
mars-c26 (scholarly-interpretation): Virility as a kind of life force (vis) or virtue (virtus) is an essential characteristic of Mars.
mars-c27 (historical-record): By the 3rd century CE, Mars was primarily worshipped by Roman military legions.
mars-c28 (historical-record): According to Servius, in wartime a spear was shaken in the Regia and Mars was called upon to watch over the Roman people with a set formula.
mars-c29 (scholarly-interpretation): Like Ares, son of Zeus and Hera, Mars is usually considered the son of Jupiter and Juno; in Ovid's version he was the son of Juno alone.
mars-c30 (traditional-belief): In Ovid's version Flora obtained a magic flower, tested it on a heifer that became fecund at once, and touched Juno's belly with it, impregnating her.
mars-c31 (scholarly-interpretation): Ovid is the only source for the story.
mars-c32 (traditional-belief): The consort of Mars was Nerio or Neriene, meaning "Valor".
mars-c33 (historical-record): In the early 3rd century BCE, the comic playwright Plautus refers to Mars greeting Nerio, his wife.
mars-c34 (historical-record): Although not originally part of the Roman tradition, in 217 BCE Venus and Mars were presented as a complementary pair in the lectisternium.
mars-c35 (etymology): The oldest recorded Latin form, Mamart-, is likely of foreign origin; it has been explained as deriving from Maris, an Etruscan child-god, though this is not universally agreed.
mars-c36 (etymology): The older etymology equating Mars with the Vedic Maruts is now rejected in more modern Indo-Europeanist scholarship.
mars-c37 (etymology): In older literature, Mars was equated with the Vedic storm deities known as the Maruts.
mars-c38 (etymology): Latin adjectives from the name of Mars mean "of Mars" or "warlike", from which the English term "martial" derives.
mars-c39 (traditional-belief): In the mythological origin of Rome outlined by the 1st-century BCE poet Virgil, Mars was cast as the ancestor of Romulus and Venus as the ancestor of Aeneas.
mars-c40 (scholarly-interpretation): Mars is considered more level-headed than the impulsive and disruptive Ares, and more virtuous to the martially minded Romans.
mars-c41 (traditional-belief): In the story their mother, the Vestal Virgin Rhea Silvia, was raped by Mars while she slept, and in a dream she dropped a hairpin that sprang up as two twin trees.
mars-c42 (traditional-belief): Anna Perenna fancied the war god for herself and double-crossed Mars by disguising herself as Minerva, veiled, tricking him into marrying her.
mars-c43 (traditional-belief): Another uniquely Roman myth is Mars's marriage to Anna Perenna; the story, retold in Ovid's Fasti, begins with Mars falling in love with Minerva.
mars-c44 (historical-record): Mars again took centre stage at the Equus October on 15 October, when a great horse race was held in the Campus Martius of Rome.
mars-c45 (historical-record): Finally the Armilustrium festival on 19 October, held on the Aventine, involved the purification of weapons before they were stored away for the winter.
mars-c46 (historical-record): The Tubilustrium involved rites to cleanse and favour trumpets, while the Equirria ensured all would be well with the horses on campaign.
mars-c47 (historical-record): Another important ritual was performed by the commander of the departing army, who shook the god's sacred spears kept in the Regia.
mars-c48 (historical-record): On 1, 9 and 23 March the priests called the Salii sang hymns and dressed in bronze-age armour including the ancient figure-of-eight ancilia shields.
mars-c49 (historical-record): Mars was also involved in ceremonies for the ancient triad of gods that included himself, Jupiter and Quirinus.
mars-c50 (historical-record): The ceremonies were carried out by Mars' specially dedicated priests, the flamen Martialis.
mars-c51 (historical-record): In Livy the Vestal was forcibly violated and gave birth to twins; she named Mars as their father, either because she believed it or because the fault might seem less heinous if a deity were the cause.
mars-c52 (historical-record): In Livy a thirsty she-wolf from the surrounding hills, drawn by the children's crying, came and suckled them and was so gentle that the king's flock-master found her licking the boys.
