
=== asclepius (lengkap-informasi, batch-036)
Asclepius | tier rich | deity | jenis dewa | greek-mythology
Ringkas: Asclepius is the Greek god of medicine, son of Apollo, who healed the sick and even revived the dead before Zeus killed him; he is recognized by his snake-entwined staff.
Kemampuan: healing — Asclepius is a healer of sicknesses who received the secrets of medicine using plants and herbs from his father. [asclepius-c01, asclepius-c24, asclepius-c33]
Kemampuan: null — He was so skilled he could restore the dead to life; according to Apollodorus he used the Gorgon's blood from Athena for this. [asclepius-c13, asclepius-c15, asclepius-c35]
Kemampuan: prophecy — Asclepius was also credited with powers of prophecy; the snakes around him were connected to prophetic powers. [asclepius-c01, asclepius-c45]
Kemampuan: null — At his sanctuaries the god was believed to effect cures or prescribe remedies to the sick in dreams. [asclepius-c07, asclepius-c32]
asclepius-c01 (religious-tradition): Asclepius is the Greek god of medicine and was credited with prophecy.
asclepius-c02 (traditional-belief): Asclepius was a son of Apollo and Koronis in one account.
asclepius-c03 (traditional-belief): The centaur Chiron taught Asclepius.
asclepius-c04 (traditional-belief): Zeus killed Asclepius with a thunderbolt.
asclepius-c05 (traditional-belief): Asclepius was deified after his death.
asclepius-c06 (historical-record): Asclepius was especially worshipped at Epidaurus.
asclepius-c07 (historical-record): At Epidaurus, patients slept awaiting dreams offering cures from the god.
asclepius-c08 (historical-record): Asclepius is depicted bearded with a snake-entwined staff, a modern medical symbol.
asclepius-c09 (historical-record): His cult was brought to Rome in snake form and given a temple on Tiber Island.
asclepius-c10 (historical-record): Epidaurus was an important healing centre in Greek and Roman times.
asclepius-c11 (religious-tradition): Asklepios was the god of medicine and also the patron god and reputed ancestor of the Asklepiades, the ancient guild of doctors.
asclepius-c12 (religious-tradition): His mother died in labour and when she was laid on the pyre Apollo cut the unborn child from her womb; from this Asklepios received his name, which means "to cut open".
asclepius-c13 (religious-tradition): He grew so skilled that he could restore the dead to life; this was a crime against the natural order, so Zeus destroyed him with a thunderbolt.
asclepius-c14 (religious-tradition): After his death Asklepios was placed among the stars as the constellation Ophiochus ("the Serpent Holder").
asclepius-c15 (religious-tradition): According to Apollodorus' summary, he received from Athena the blood from the Gorgon's veins: the left side he used to destroy people, the right to preserve them, which is how he could revive the dead.
asclepius-c16 (religious-tradition): Zeus was afraid men might learn medicine from Asklepios and help each other, so he struck him with a thunderbolt; this angered Apollo, who slew the Cyclopes who designed the thunderbolt for Zeus.
asclepius-c17 (scholarly-interpretation): These temples were not only places of worship but were frequented by great numbers of sick persons and may therefore be compared to modern hospitals.
asclepius-c18 (historical-record): The principal seat of his worship in Greece was Epidaurus, where his temple was surrounded by an extensive grove within which no one was allowed to die and no woman to give birth.
asclepius-c19 (scholarly-interpretation): Serpents were connected with the worship of Aesculapius everywhere, probably as a symbol of prudence and renovation, and were believed able to discover herbs of wondrous powers.
asclepius-c20 (historical-record): At Epidaurus he was shown seated on a throne, holding a staff in one hand and resting the other on the head of a dragon (serpent), with a dog by his side.
asclepius-c21 (historical-record): Medical knowledge was regarded as a sacred secret transmitted from father to son in the families of the Asclepiadae.
asclepius-c22 (scholarly-interpretation): In the Homeric poems Aesculapius does not appear to be considered a divinity, but merely a human being.
asclepius-c23 (historical-record): Asklepios was depicted as a kindly, bearded man holding a serpent-entwined staff.
asclepius-c24 (religious-tradition): Homeric Hymn XVI sings of Asclepius, son of Apollo and healer of sicknesses.
asclepius-c25 (religious-tradition): According to the Hymn, Coronis, daughter of King Phlegyas, bore him in the Dotian plain, a great joy to men and a soother of cruel pangs.
asclepius-c26 (historical-record): All who were healed offered sacrifice, especially a cock, and hung up votive tablets recording their names, their diseases and how they had been cured.
asclepius-c27 (religious-tradition): Aesculapius was the legendary Greek god of medicine, son of Apollo and the nymph Coronis; Tricca in Thessaly and Epidaurus in Argolis disputed the honour of his birthplace, but an oracle declared for Epidaurus.
asclepius-c28 (religious-tradition): His skill in curing disease and restoring the dead to life aroused Zeus's anger; fearing he might make all men immortal, Zeus slew him with a thunderbolt.
asclepius-c29 (historical-record): He is commonly shown standing in a long cloak with bare breast, with a club-like staff coiled by a serpent (symbol of renovation) as his usual attribute; he is often accompanied by Telesphorus, the boy genius of healing, and his daughter Hygieia, goddess of health.
asclepius-c30 (historical-record): His worship was introduced into Rome by order of the Sibylline books (293 B.C.) to avert a pestilence.
asclepius-c31 (historical-record): Temples to Aesculapius were erected in many parts of Greece, near healing springs or on high mountains.
asclepius-c32 (religious-tradition): The practice of sleeping (incubatio) in these sanctuaries was very common, it being supposed that the god effected cures or prescribed remedies to the sick in dreams.
asclepius-c33 (religious-tradition): The motherless Asclepius was brought up by his father, who gave him the gift of healing and the secrets of medicine using plants and herbs.
asclepius-c34 (religious-tradition): Asclepius had many children: two sons, Machaon and Podaleirios, and four daughters, Iaso, Panacea, Aceso and Aglaia.
asclepius-c35 (religious-tradition): The father of the gods saw Asclepius and his healing art as a threat to the eternal division between humanity and the gods, especially after rumours that his healing powers were so formidable he could even raise the dead.
asclepius-c36 (religious-tradition): In some accounts Koronis abandoned her child near Epidaurus and the baby was looked after by a goat and a dog; in another Koronis was killed by Apollo for being unfaithful; in yet another the Messenian Arsinoe was his mother.
asclepius-c37 (historical-record): Epidaurus was also the site of the pan-Hellenic Asklepieia festival, founded in the 5th century BCE and held every four years to celebrate theatre, sport and music in honour of Asclepius.
asclepius-c38 (historical-record): Another important sanctuary of Asclepius was in Athens, just below the Acropolis on the western slope; tradition said a priest named Telemachos brought the god there in the form of a sacred snake in 419 BCE.
asclepius-c39 (historical-record): In 293 BCE the Romans were said to have taken the sacred snake from Epidaurus to the Tiber Island in order to cure a plague.
asclepius-c40 (historical-record): The sanctuary finally closed in 426 CE when the Roman emperor Theodosius II decreed the closure of all pagan sites in Greece.
asclepius-c41 (historical-record): Asclepius's descendants who continued in medicine were known as the Asclepiads; Machaon helped the wounded Menelaos in the Trojan War, but the most famous doctor of the family was undoubtedly Hippocrates.
asclepius-c42 (historical-record): He is sometimes accompanied by Hygeia and occasionally has a dog at his feet, as these animals were sacred at some of his sanctuaries.
asclepius-c43 (religious-tradition): Apollo protested against his son's treatment but was himself punished by Zeus for impiety and made to serve Admetos, king of Thessaly, for one year.
asclepius-c44 (historical-record): Thankful patients often left votive offerings at the site, sometimes depicting the body part that had been cured.
asclepius-c45 (scholarly-interpretation): Snakes were associated with Asclepius and symbolised regeneration, as they were thought to live both below and above ground, and were connected to prophetic powers as they knew the hidden secrets below ground.
asclepius-c46 (religious-tradition): In some traditions he was married to Hygeia, also a goddess of health; in another version she was his daughter and Asclepius married Epione.
asclepius-c47 (historical-record): The cult of Asclepius was also transferred to Pergamon sometime in the 4th century BCE, possibly by a healed patient at Epidaurus named Archias.
asclepius-c48 (scholarly-interpretation): The serpent, Aesculapius's perpetual symbol, has given rise to the opinion that his worship was derived from Egypt and that he was identical with the serpent Cnuph worshipped in Egypt or the Phoenician Esmun.
asclepius-c49 (religious-tradition): According to others, the cause was that Pluto had complained that Aesculapius diminished the number of the dead too much.
asclepius-c50 (scholarly-interpretation): Asklepios' apotheosis into godhood occurred at the same time; he was sometimes identified with Homer's Paion (Paeon), the physician of the gods.
asclepius-c51 (historical-record): Here was the god's most famous shrine, and games were celebrated in his honour every five years, accompanied by solemn processions.
asclepius-c52 (religious-tradition): The sanctuary at Epidaurus was known as the Asklepieion because he was believed to have been born on the nearby Mt. Titthion.
