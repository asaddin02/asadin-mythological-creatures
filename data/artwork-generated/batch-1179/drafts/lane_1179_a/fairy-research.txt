
=== fairy (lengkap-informasi, batch-037)
Fairy | tier rich | fairy | jenis peri | tradition-british
Ringkas: Fairies are supernatural beings of folklore, especially in Britain and Ireland, with varied forms and behaviour: some are human-sized, some small, and their conduct can be dangerous or benevolent.
Kemampuan: magic — Fairies are described as magically intermeddling in human affairs and as having supernatural powers. [fairy-c01, fairy-c04]
Kemampuan: shapeshifting — British and Irish fairies are described as able to vanish, shape-shift and create illusions. [fairy-c04]
Kemampuan: immortality — In British and Irish stories, all fairies are described as immortal. [fairy-c04]
Kemampuan: flight — Some fairies fly without wings, mounting twigs or stems, wearing a special cap or uttering potent words. [fairy-c07]
fairy-c01 (traditional-belief): Fairy refers to supernatural beings said to interfere magically in human affairs.
fairy-c02 (historical-record): Fairies appear in old British and Irish folk narratives.
fairy-c03 (scholarly-interpretation): Fairies are split between numerous local species, from full-sized Scottish elf-queens to small, wizened male house fairies.
fairy-c04 (traditional-belief): In British and Irish tales, fairies are immortal and can vanish, shapeshift, or create illusions.
fairy-c05 (traditional-belief): Fairies may be dangerous or benevolent depending on human conduct in stories.
fairy-c06 (traditional-belief): Fairy stories include child abduction and changeling substitution.
fairy-c07 (traditional-belief): In older stories some fairies fly without wings.
fairy-c08 (historical-record): Fairy lore has been studied in Folklore since the late nineteenth century.
fairy-c09 (traditional-belief): The "people of peace" (Daoine Shie) of Ireland and Scotland are usually of ordinary stature and not recognisable as differing from mankind except by their proceedings.
fairy-c10 (etymology): The 1911 Britannica traces the word fairy (and its cognates fée, fada, hada, fata) to Medieval Latin fatare, "to enchant", from Latin fatum, fate or destiny.
fairy-c11 (scholarly-interpretation): Andrew Lang judges fairies the most beautiful, most numerous and most memorable in literature of the minor creatures of mythology.
fairy-c12 (traditional-belief): In Irish belief, fairies dwell in the "raths", old earth-forts, and in subterranean houses, which are also common in Scotland.
fairy-c13 (traditional-belief): Irish fairies are described as an organized people, often called "the army", whose life corresponds to human life in all particulars.
fairy-c14 (traditional-belief): A living human being must not eat in Fairyland; if he does, he dwells there for ever.
fairy-c15 (traditional-belief): In one report, neighbours said fairies caused flying stones because the man had swept his chimney with a bough of holly, "a gentle tree" dear to the fairies.
fairy-c16 (traditional-belief): In Ireland and the West Highlands, Neolithic arrow-heads and flint chips are still regarded as fairy weapons.
fairy-c17 (scholarly-interpretation): Lang writes that the nearest analogy to the form of the belief in Scotland and Ireland, and the "pixies" of south-western England, is found in the jān or jinn of Arabs, Moors and people of Palestine.
fairy-c18 (scholarly-interpretation): Lang concludes that fairies and jān are practically identical.
fairy-c19 (historical-record): Fairies entered the poetry of the Middle Ages; they take lovers from among men and are often described as delicately, unearthly beautiful.
fairy-c20 (historical-record): In the tale of Melusine, the fay built the Castle of Lusignan by enchantment for her husband and each week took the form of a serpent from the waist down.
fairy-c21 (scholarly-interpretation): The fairies of medieval romance differ little from human beings except in supernatural knowledge and power, and are not often shown as diminutive.
fairy-c22 (scholarly-interpretation): Lang mentions fairy women who come to the births of children and foretell their fortunes.
fairy-c23 (scholarly-interpretation): Lang mentions the theory that fairies survive from prehistoric memories of a pigmy people in subterranean earth-houses, but notes the contents of these do not point to an age before the close of the Roman occupation of Britain.
fairy-c24 (historical-record): Gervaise of Tilbury, writing in the early 13th century in his Otia Imperialia, reports that men have been lovers of beings they call fadas.
fairy-c25 (scholarly-interpretation): Keightley divides the beings called fairies into two classes; the first are beings of the human race endowed with powers beyond those usually allotted to men, whom he terms fays, or fairies of romance.
fairy-c26 (scholarly-interpretation): The second class, in Keightley's scheme, are the little beings of popular creeds, whose descent he proposes to trace from the Duergar or dwarfs of Northern mythology, and whom he calls elves or popular fairies.
fairy-c27 (scholarly-interpretation): Keightley concludes that the root of the Romance words fée, fada, hada and fata is the Latin fatum.
fairy-c28 (scholarly-interpretation): Keightley writes that no theory is so plausible, or supported by such names, as the one deriving English fairy from Persian peri.
fairy-c29 (historical-record): The word came to mean the individual inhabitant of Fairy-land, applied both to the full-sized fairy knights and ladies of romance and to the pygmy elves of woods and dells.
fairy-c30 (scholarly-interpretation): Keightley writes that such beings generally excel mankind in power and knowledge and, like humans, are subject to death, though after a longer span of existence.
fairy-c31 (scholarly-interpretation): Keightley writes that after the appearance of the Faerie Queene all distinctions were confounded and the name and attributes of the fays of romance were transferred to the little beings.
fairy-c32 (traditional-belief): Lang writes that the herb rue is potent against the jān, as in British folk-lore.
