
=== jupiter (lengkap-informasi, batch-028)
Jupiter | tier rich | deity | jenis dewa | tradition-roman
Ringkas: Jupiter is the sky and thunder god and king of the gods in ancient Roman religion, with the thunderbolt and eagle as his emblems.
Kemampuan: elemental-control — Jupiter is seen as wielder of lightning and master of weather. The thunderbolt is his identifying implement, and in Roman accounts he punishes with lightning. [jupiter-c03, jupiter-c23, jupiter-c32]
Kemampuan: null — Jupiter communicates with people through thunder, lightning, and the flight of birds, called his auspices; the eagle held precedence in taking auspices. [jupiter-c03, jupiter-c24]
Kemampuan: null — Jupiter could punish people with his thunderbolts, but gave warning first and usually acted with the consent of other gods. In Ovid's Metamorphoses he destroys Lycaon's dwelling with thunderbolts. [jupiter-c13, jupiter-c37]
jupiter-c01 (religious-tradition): Jupiter is god of the sky and thunder and king of the gods.
jupiter-c02 (historical-record): Jupiter was the leading deity of Roman state religion in the Republican and Imperial eras.
jupiter-c03 (historical-record): The thunderbolt and eagle are Jupiter's emblems.
jupiter-c04 (historical-record): Romans viewed Jupiter as a counterpart of Greek Zeus.
jupiter-c05 (historical-record): The title Jupiter Optimus Maximus expressed Jupiter's supreme status.
jupiter-c06 (historical-record): Jupiter was held to protect Rome in war and preserve welfare in peace.
jupiter-c07 (historical-record): Jupiter is depicted with a white beard and an eagle on his scepter.
jupiter-c08 (historical-record): North African Ammon was equated with Jupiter as Jupiter Ammon.
jupiter-c09 (historical-record): Among the many Roman gods, Jupiter, son of Saturn, was the supreme god, associated with thunder, lightning, and storms.
jupiter-c10 (historical-record): The early citizens of Rome added a triad of gods consisting of Mars, Quirinus, and Jupiter as the supreme god.
jupiter-c11 (historical-record): By the Republic, Jupiter was firmly established as the greatest god, and two members of the old triad were replaced by Juno (his sister and wife) and Minerva (his daughter).
jupiter-c12 (historical-record): Jupiter was seen as a god of light, protector in defeat, and giver of victory, with the titles Imperator, Invictus, and Triumphator.
jupiter-c13 (historical-record): Jupiter had a reputation for violence: he could punish people with a thunderbolt, but gave a warning first, and punishment was usually carried out with the consent of the other gods.
jupiter-c14 (historical-record): Around 509 BCE a magnificent temple shared with Juno and Minerva was built on Capitoline Hill in Rome; it housed a large sculpture of Jupiter and the Sibylline books.
jupiter-c15 (historical-record): Victorious generals led a triumph procession to Jupiter's temple, made a sacrifice, and donated part of their booty to Jupiter in thanks for victory.
jupiter-c16 (historical-record): The Senate would not permit a declaration of war without Jupiter's blessing, and he was patron of oaths and treaties and punisher of perjurers.
jupiter-c17 (historical-record): No political action was begun or completed without his judgment, and the Ludi Romani, the chief games held in September, honored him.
jupiter-c18 (historical-record): Emperor Elagabalus temporarily replaced Jupiter with Elagabal, a Syrian god.
jupiter-c19 (historical-record): In the Capitoline Triad he was the central guardian of the state with Juno and Minerva; his sacred tree was the oak.
jupiter-c20 (historical-record): In the Greek-influenced tradition Jupiter was brother of Neptune and Pluto, each presiding over one of the three realms of the universe: sky, waters, and underworld.
jupiter-c21 (etymology): The Latin name Iūpiter or Iuppiter derives from Proto-Italic *djous "day, sky" plus *patēr "father", thus "sky father".
jupiter-c22 (etymology): Jove is a less common English formation based on Iov-, the stem of the oblique cases of the Latin name.
jupiter-c23 (scholarly-interpretation): Jupiter took on atmospheric qualities: he is the wielder of lightning and master of weather.
jupiter-c24 (scholarly-interpretation): Jupiter communicates with people through thunder, lightning, and the flight of birds (his auspices), and guards public oaths and compacts and guarantees good faith in the state cult.
jupiter-c25 (scholarly-interpretation): Wissowa considered Jupiter also a god of war and agriculture, besides his political role as guarantor of good faith.
jupiter-c26 (scholarly-interpretation): Dumézil maintains that Jupiter is not himself a god of war and agriculture, although his actions and interests may extend to those spheres.
jupiter-c27 (traditional-belief): The Romans believed Jupiter granted them supremacy because they had honoured him more than any other people had.
jupiter-c28 (historical-record): The consuls swore their oath of office in Jupiter's name and honoured him at the annual feriae of the Capitol in September, sacrificing a white ox with gilded horns.
jupiter-c29 (etymology): Jove was the original namesake of the Latin forms of the weekday now called Thursday in English (originally Iovis Dies in Latin).
jupiter-c30 (scholarly-interpretation): Tinia is usually regarded as his Etruscan counterpart.
jupiter-c31 (traditional-belief): In Roman mythology he negotiates with Numa Pompilius, the second king of Rome, to establish principles of Roman religion such as offering or sacrifice.
jupiter-c32 (traditional-belief): According to Livy, Tullus found secret rites for Jupiter Elicius but performed them wrongly; Jupiter was angered and burnt up the king and his house with a stroke of lightning.
jupiter-c33 (traditional-belief): In Livy's account Romulus vows a temple to "Jove the Stayer" (Jupiter Stator) as a memorial that the City was saved through Jupiter's help.
jupiter-c34 (traditional-belief): Romulus then cries out that Jupiter Optimus Maximus bids the Romans stand and renew the battle.
jupiter-c35 (traditional-belief): In Livy's account, after leading his victorious army home, the leader mounted to the Capitol with the spoils of his dead foe, hung them on an oak the shepherds held sacred, and marked out the site for Jupiter's temple.
jupiter-c36 (traditional-belief): In Livy's account Romulus addresses the god by a new title, Jupiter Feretrius, offers the arms taken from a king, and dedicates a temple to receive the spolia opima.
jupiter-c37 (traditional-belief): In Ovid's Metamorphoses Jupiter destroys Lycaon's dwelling with thunderbolts as vengeance.
