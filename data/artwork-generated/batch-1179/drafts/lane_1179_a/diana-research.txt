diana-c01 (religious-tradition): Diana was a Roman goddess associated with childbirth, fertility, the moon, and wild animals.
diana-c02 (religious-tradition): Diana is best known as a hunt goddess, with the deer as her sacred animal.
diana-c03 (scholarly-interpretation): Diana was identified with the Greek Artemis.
diana-c04 (traditional-belief): According to World History Encyclopedia, in Roman genealogy Diana is the daughter of Jupiter and Latona.
diana-c05 (traditional-belief): Diana and Apollo were said to be born on Delos.
diana-c06 (historical-record): Roman art commonly portrays Diana with a bow and quiver.
diana-c07 (traditional-belief): Actaeon appears in one of Diana's best-known stories.
diana-c08 (traditional-belief): In the Actaeon story he grows the horns of a stag, his hands become feet and his arms are changed into legs.
diana-c09 (historical-record): An important Roman sanctuary of Diana stood at Aricia by Lake Nemi.
diana-c10 (historical-record): A Met bronze statue depicts Artemis, known to Romans as Diana.
diana-c11 (traditional-belief): According to most accounts, Diana's skill with the bow equaled that of her twin brother Apollo.
diana-c12 (traditional-belief): In the Niobe story, an enraged Latona told Apollo and Diana to kill all 14 of Niobe's children.
diana-c13 (traditional-belief): Choosing never to marry, Diana received her father's permission to remain a virgin, joining Vesta and Minerva as the only virgin goddesses.
diana-c14 (traditional-belief): In the Endymion story, Diana caused the youth to fall into an eternal sleep, fearing his beauty might be marred by toil and exposure.
diana-c15 (traditional-belief): In the Orion story, Diana hit a target that turned out to be Orion swimming at night, and in her grief turned Orion and Sirius into a constellation.
diana-c16 (traditional-belief): When the nymphs asked Diana for help, she turned them into pigeons.
diana-c17 (traditional-belief): In the Trojan War story, an angered Diana stilled the waters so that the Greek ships could not sail.
diana-c18 (traditional-belief): When Iphigenia was about to be sacrificed, Diana intervened and replaced her with a deer; she later became a priestess at Diana's temple.
diana-c19 (traditional-belief): In the Aeneid, Diana is heartbroken at the death of Camilla, a favorite of hers.
diana-c20 (scholarly-interpretation): According to the 1911 Britannica, in Roman mythology Diana is an old Italian goddess, in later times identified with the Greek Artemis.
diana-c21 (etymology): Her name, the feminine form of Janus (Dianus), shows she was originally an independent Italian deity.
diana-c22 (scholarly-interpretation): According to the 1911 Britannica, Diana is essentially the goddess of the moon and light generally, presiding over wood, plain and water, the chase and war.
diana-c23 (historical-record): As goddess of childbirth Diana was known, like Juno, as Lucina, the "bringer to light".
diana-c24 (historical-record): As moon-goddess Diana was also identified with Hecate and invoked as "three-formed" in reference to the phases of the moon.
diana-c25 (historical-record): Her most celebrated shrine was in a grove at Aricia near Lake Nemi (hence her title Nemorensis), where she was worshipped alongside Virbius, a god of the forest and the chase.
diana-c26 (historical-record): Diana's priest at Aricia, called Rex Nemorensis, was a runaway slave obliged to qualify for office by slaying his predecessor in single combat.
diana-c27 (historical-record): The worship of Diana was brought to Rome by Latin plebeians, so she was regarded as protectress of the lower classes and especially of slaves.
diana-c28 (historical-record): Diana's most important temple at Rome was on the Aventine, chief seat of the plebeians, founded by Servius Tullius originally as a sanctuary of the Latin league.
diana-c29 (historical-record): No man was allowed to enter the temple, and on its dedication day (August 13) the slaves kept holiday.
diana-c30 (historical-record): The goddess's festival was on the ides (13th) of August; she was worshipped with torches, and women sought her aid for a happy delivery in childbirth.
diana-c31 (historical-record): As Noctiluca, "giving light by night", Diana had a sanctuary on the Palatine kept illuminated all night.
diana-c32 (historical-record): Another celebrated sanctuary of Diana was on the slopes of Mount Tifata near Capua, where she was worshipped as Tifatina.
diana-c33 (traditional-belief): In Ovid's Metamorphoses the valley of Gargaphia is sacred to Diana, dense with pine trees and pointed cypress.
diana-c34 (traditional-belief): In Ovid, Diana, the Sylvan goddess, loved to bathe in the crystal pool of that valley when wearied with sport.
diana-c35 (traditional-belief): In Ovid's Actaeon story, Diana wished her arrows were at hand but, having only water, dashed it on the young man's face.
diana-c36 (traditional-belief): In Ovid, Diana fixed a great stag's horns on Actaeon's brows, lengthened his neck, made his ears sharp, and changed his hands and feet.
diana-c37 (traditional-belief): In Ovid it is said that Diana's fury did not abate until the torn flesh ceased to live.
diana-c38 (traditional-belief): In Ovid, Diana tells Actaeon to tell, if his tongue can, that his bold eyes saw her stripped of all her robes.
diana-c39 (traditional-belief): In Ovid, while the nymphs bathed Diana, Actaeon, wandering through unknown woods, entered the precincts of that sacred grove.
