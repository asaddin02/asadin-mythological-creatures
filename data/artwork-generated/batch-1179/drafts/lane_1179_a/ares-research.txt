ares-c01 (religious-tradition): Ares is a Greek god of war and courage, an Olympian and son of Zeus and Hera.
ares-c02 (religious-tradition): Ares is an Olympian deity associated with war and courage.
ares-c03 (historical-record): Ancient art shows Ares armored with helmet, shield, and spear.
ares-c04 (religious-tradition): Hephaistos trapped Ares and Aphrodite in a golden net.
ares-c05 (religious-tradition): Twin giants confined Ares in a bronze vessel until Hermes freed him.
ares-c06 (historical-record): Romans identified Mars with Ares.
ares-c07 (historical-record): Ancient depictions show Ares as a bearded warrior or a helmeted youth with spear.
ares-c08 (etymology): The etymology of the name Ares is traditionally connected with the Greek word ἀρή (arē), the Ionic form of Doric ἀρά (ara), "bane, ruin, curse, imprecation".
ares-c09 (scholarly-interpretation): Ares's name shows Mycenaean origins, but his reputation for savagery was thought by some to reflect his likely origins as a Thracian deity.
ares-c10 (scholarly-interpretation): Many Greeks were ambivalent toward Ares: he embodies the physical valor needed for success in war but can also personify sheer brutality and bloodlust, in contrast to his sister Athena, whose martial functions include strategy and generalship.
ares-c11 (scholarly-interpretation): An association with Ares endows places, objects, and other deities with a savage, dangerous, or militarized quality.
ares-c12 (scholarly-interpretation): Although there are many literary allusions to Ares's love affairs and children, he has a limited role in Greek mythology, and when he appears he is often humiliated.
ares-c13 (historical-record): Enyalios was sometimes identified with Ares and sometimes differentiated from him as another war god with a separate cult, even in the same town.
ares-c14 (historical-record): There was an archaic Spartan statue of Ares in chains in the temple of Enyalios, which Pausanias claimed meant the spirit of war and victory was to be kept in the city.
ares-c15 (historical-record): Some cities in Greece and several in Asia Minor held annual festivals to bind and detain him as their protector, and in parts of Asia Minor he was an oracular deity.
ares-c16 (historical-record): Further from Greece, the Scythians were said to ritually kill one in a hundred prisoners of war as an offering to their equivalent of Ares.
ares-c17 (scholarly-interpretation): The later belief that ancient Spartans offered human sacrifice to Ares may owe more to mythical prehistory, misunderstandings, and reputation than to reality.
ares-c18 (traditional-belief): In the Trojan War, Aphrodite, protector of Troy, persuades Ares to take the Trojans' side; the Trojans lose while Athena, Ares's sister, helps the Greeks to victory.
ares-c19 (historical-record): Ares's nearest counterpart in Roman religion is Mars, who was given a more important and dignified place as ancestral protector of the Roman people and state.
ares-c20 (historical-record): In Africa, Maḥrem, the principal god of the kings of Aksum before the 4th century AD, was invoked as Ares in Greek inscriptions.
ares-c21 (traditional-belief): Thrace was considered Ares's birthplace and his refuge after the affair with Aphrodite was exposed to the mockery of the other gods.
ares-c22 (historical-record): Like most Greek deities, Ares received animal sacrifice; in Sparta after battle he was given an ox for a victory by stratagem or a rooster for victory through onslaught.
ares-c23 (scholarly-interpretation): In Renaissance and Neoclassical literary works Ares is replaced by the Roman Mars, a romantic emblem of manly valor rather than the cruel and blood-thirsty god of Greek mythology.
ares-c24 (historical-record): His cults in southern Asia Minor are attested from the 5th century BC well into the Roman Imperial era, at 29 sites and on over 70 local coin issues.
ares-c25 (traditional-belief): The Areopagus ("mount of Ares"), a natural rock outcrop in Athens some distance from the Acropolis, was supposedly where Ares was tried and acquitted by the gods for the revenge-killing of Poseidon's son Halirrhothius, who had raped Ares's daughter Alcippe.
ares-c26 (historical-record): Ares' main attribute was a peaked warrior's helm, even in domestic scenes such as feasts of the gods; his other attributes included a shield, a spear, and sometimes a sheathed sword.
ares-c27 (scholarly-interpretation): Ares can be quite difficult to identify in ancient Greek art because little distinguishes him from other warrior figures.
ares-c28 (historical-record): Ares' sacred animal was the serpent; he was also associated with certain birds such as the vulture and a few species of owl, which ancient augury identified as portents of war, sedition, and ill-fortune.
ares-c29 (traditional-belief): The most famous of the god's animals in myth were the Colchian Dragon, a serpent set by Ares to guard the Golden Fleece, and the Ismenian Dragon, a giant snake guarding his sacred spring near Thebes.
ares-c30 (traditional-belief): Ares was a son of Zeus and Hera, King and Queen of the Gods, and brother of the goddesses Eileithyia and Hebe.
ares-c31 (traditional-belief): Ares had three children by Aphrodite named Deimos (Fear), Phobos (Terror), and Harmonia (Harmony).
ares-c32 (traditional-belief): When Aphrodite fell in love with the handsome youth Adonis, Ares grew jealous, transformed himself into a boar, and gored the boy to death while he was hunting.
ares-c33 (traditional-belief): Ares transformed his daughter Harmonia and her husband Kadmos of Thebes into serpents and had them carried away to the Islands of the Blessed.
ares-c34 (traditional-belief): Ares slew Hallirhothios to avenge the rape of his daughter Alkippe; he was tried at the court of the Areiopagos in Athens but acquitted of murder.
ares-c35 (traditional-belief): In the battle between Herakles and Ares' villainous son Kyknos, the god intervened but was wounded by the hero and forced to flee back to Olympos.
ares-c36 (traditional-belief): Ares actively supported his Amazon-queen daughters in their many wars and battles; the most celebrated was Penthesileia, who joined the Trojan War.
ares-c37 (traditional-belief): During the Trojan War, Ares, who had sided with the Trojans, was wounded by the Greek hero Diomedes, who drove a spear into his side, sending him flying back to Olympos bellowing in pain.
ares-c38 (scholarly-interpretation): According to this dictionary, Ares is nothing but the personification of bold force and strength, less the god of war than of its tumult, confusion, and horrors; Eris calls forth war, Zeus directs its course, but Ares loves war for its own sake.
ares-c39 (scholarly-interpretation): The warlike character of the Thracian tribes led to the belief that the god's residence was in that country, and there and in Scythia were the principal seats of his worship.
ares-c40 (scholarly-interpretation): The temples of this god were usually built outside the towns, probably to suggest that he was to prevent enemies from approaching them.
ares-c41 (traditional-belief): In Hesiod's Shield of Heracles, Ares is shown holding a spear, urging on the footmen, red with blood, and standing in his chariot.
ares-c42 (traditional-belief): Homeric Hymn 8 addresses Ares as exceeding in strength, chariot-rider, golden-helmed, shield-bearer, saviour of cities, harnessed in bronze, and mighty with the spear.
ares-c43 (scholarly-interpretation): Ares was the Greek god of war and perhaps the most unpopular of the Olympian gods because of his quick temper, aggressiveness, and unquenchable thirst for conflict.
ares-c44 (historical-record): Described by Hesiod in the Theogony as "shield-piercing Ares" and "city-sacking Ares", he represented the more brutal and bloody side of battle, in contrast to Athena, who represented its more strategic elements.
ares-c45 (traditional-belief): Ares' son Kyknos waylaid pilgrims to the oracle at Delphi, so Apollo sent Hercules; Hercules killed Kyknos, a furious Ares fought him, but Hercules was protected by Athena and even managed to wound Ares.
ares-c46 (traditional-belief): In Homer's version of the Trojan War in the Iliad, Ares supports the Trojans, sometimes even leading them in battle along with Hector.
ares-c47 (traditional-belief): Ares is roundly beaten by Athena, who, supporting the Achaeans, knocks him out with a large rock.
ares-c48 (traditional-belief): Fleeing back to Olympus, Ares' complaints are ignored by Zeus, who nonetheless instructs Paieon to heal his wound.
ares-c49 (historical-record): Ares was not commonly worshipped, but there were cult sites with temples on Crete (he is mentioned in Linear B tablets from Knossos) and at Argos, Athens, Erythrae, Geronthrae, Megalopolis, Tegea, Therapne, and Troezen, and he also had a cult in Thrace.
ares-c50 (historical-record): Ares is sometimes shown riding a chariot pulled by fire-breathing horses; the myth of his battle with Hercules was a popular subject for Attic vases in the 6th century BCE.
ares-c51 (historical-record): In Roman mythology Mars was also the father of Romulus and Remus, the legendary founders of Rome, and the city therefore achieved a sacred status.
ares-c52 (traditional-belief): In Greek myths Ares was usually found in the company of his children by Aphrodite, Phobos and Deimos, his sister Eris (Strife), and his charioteer Ennyo.
ares-c53 (traditional-belief): Hephaistos managed to entrap the lovers in an ingenious bed, a tale told in some detail in Book 8 of Homer's Odyssey; Ares' punishment was temporary banishment from Mount Olympus.
ares-c54 (traditional-belief): In the Iliad, Diomedes drives his bronze spear at Ares and Athene speeds it against Ares' lower belly where he is girded with his taslets; when the spear is drawn out, Ares bellows.
ares-c55 (traditional-belief): In the Iliad Ares' bellow is as loud as nine or ten thousand warriors crying in battle, and trembling comes upon Achaeans and Trojans alike.
ares-c56 (traditional-belief): In the Iliad, Otus and Ephialtes, sons of Aloeus, bind Ares in cruel bonds and he lies bound in a brazen jar for thirteen months, and would have perished.
ares-c57 (scholarly-interpretation): A later tradition that Hera conceived Ares by touching a certain flower appears to imitate the legend of the birth of Hephaestus and is related by Ovid.
ares-c58 (scholarly-interpretation): According to this dictionary, when Aphrodite loved Adonis, Ares in his jealousy metamorphosed himself into a bear and killed his rival.
ares-c59 (etymology): Ares is written Ἄρης in Ancient Greek, transliterated Árēs, and is the Greek god of war and courage.
ares-c60 (historical-record): Ares was one of the Twelve Olympians in the archaic tradition represented by the Iliad and Odyssey.
