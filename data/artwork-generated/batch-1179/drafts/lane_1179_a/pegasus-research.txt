
=== pegasus (lengkap-informasi, batch-035)
Pegasus | tier rich | legendary-creature | jenis hewan mitos | greek-mythology
Ringkas: Pegasus is the immortal winged horse of Greek mythology, born from Medusa's neck, ridden by Bellerophon and later bearer of Zeus's thunderbolts.
Kemampuan: flight — Bellerophon rode and flew Pegasus, and Pegasos flew on to Olympos. [pegasus-c03, pegasus-c16]

pegasus-c01 (traditional-belief): Pegasus is a winged horse of Greek mythology, fathered by Poseidon and born when Perseus beheaded Medusa.
pegasus-c02 (traditional-belief): Pegasus is an immortal winged horse that emerged from Medusa's neck.
pegasus-c03 (traditional-belief): With Athena's help, Bellerophon tamed Pegasus and rode him against the Chimaera.
pegasus-c04 (traditional-belief): Pegasus threw Bellerophon when the hero tried to fly to the gods.
pegasus-c05 (traditional-belief): In Hesiod's Theogony, Pegasus brought thunder and lightning to Zeus.
pegasus-c06 (traditional-belief): Pegasus's hoofstamp was credited with creating the Hippocrene spring on Mount Helicon.
pegasus-c07 (traditional-belief): Pegasus was commemorated as the constellation of the same name.
pegasus-c08 (historical-record): Pegasus appeared on ancient Greek pottery and coins.
pegasus-c09 (traditional-belief): At the same time and in the same way, Chrysaor (spelled Chryasor in the source) was also born.
pegasus-c10 (traditional-belief): Poseidon gave Pegasus to his son Bellerophon, who put Pegasus to good use in his famous battle with the Chimaera.
pegasus-c11 (etymology): In some accounts Bellerophon found the horse at the fountain of Pirene near Corinth, and Hesiod suggests this explains the name Pegasus, derived from pēgē ('water').
pegasus-c12 (traditional-belief): Pegasus flew on and, on reaching Mt. Olympus, was given to Eos, who was responsible for bringing Dawn across the sky each day.
pegasus-c13 (historical-record): A famous sculptural representation comes from the pediment of the Temple of Artemis on Corcyra (c. 580 BCE).
pegasus-c14 (historical-record): The Bellerophon and Pegasus myth was also popular in Roman art, especially engraved semi-precious stone cameos and floor mosaics, where the horse became symbolic of immortality.
pegasus-c15 (etymology): According to Theoi, Pegasos' name means either 'of the spring' or 'sprung forth'; the first alludes to the steed's link with various springs, the second to its birth from the Gorgon's neck.
pegasus-c16 (traditional-belief): Later the hero tried to fly to heaven, but Zeus made the horse buck and throw him back to earth; Pegasos flew on to Olympos and became Zeus's thunderbolt-bearer.
pegasus-c17 (traditional-belief): The rising of the Pegasus constellation marks the arrival of spring and, in Greece, of seasonal thunderstorms.
pegasus-c18 (scholarly-interpretation): On the view that seems the most ancient, Pegasus was the thundering horse of Zeus, whereas later writers describe him as the horse of Eos.
pegasus-c19 (traditional-belief): In one account Athena appeared to Bellerophon in a dream, told him to sacrifice to Poseidon, and gave him a golden bridle.
pegasus-c20 (traditional-belief): According to some accounts, Athena herself tamed and bridled Pegasus and handed him over to Bellerophon.
pegasus-c21 (traditional-belief): After conquering the Chimaera, he tried to rise to heaven on his winged horse but fell to earth, whether from fear or giddiness or because Pegasus, enraged by a gad-fly Zeus had sent, threw him.
pegasus-c22 (scholarly-interpretation): Pegasus was also later regarded as the horse of the Muses; in this role he is more celebrated in modern times than in antiquity, since for the ancients his only link to the Muses was that his hoof called forth the Hippocrene well.
pegasus-c23 (traditional-belief): Hesiod, Theogony: when Perseus cut off Medusa's head, Khrysaor and the horse Pegasos sprang from her blood; Pegasos was named after the springs (pegai) of Okeanos where he was born.
pegasus-c24 (historical-record): Theoi classes Hesiod's Theogony, which contains the account of Pegasos's birth, as Greek epic of the 8th or 7th century BCE.
pegasus-c25 (traditional-belief): Theoi lists Poseidon and Medousa as Pegasos's parents according to Hesiod, Apollodorus, Hyginus, and Ovid.
pegasus-c26 (traditional-belief): Theoi also records a tradition (Pindar, Nonnus) naming only Medousa as Pegasos's parent.
pegasus-c27 (traditional-belief): Hesiod (Catalogues of Women): Bellerophon's father Poseidon gave him Pegasos, who bore him swiftly on his wings and flew unwearying everywhere over the earth.
pegasus-c28 (traditional-belief): Hesiod, Theogony: Khimaira (Chimera) was killed by Pegasos and gallant Bellerophon.
pegasus-c29 (traditional-belief): Pindar: Bellerophon long strove to yoke Pegasos, the snake-haired Gorgo's offspring, until Pallas (Athena) brought him the bridle and golden headband.
pegasus-c30 (traditional-belief): Antoninus Liberalis: Mount Helikon, beguiled by the pleasure of the Muses' song, swelled skyward until Pegasos, by Poseidon's will, checked it by striking the summit with his hoof.
pegasus-c31 (traditional-belief): Pausanias: Hippokrene was made, they say, by Pegasos, the horse of Bellerophon, striking the ground with his hoof.
pegasus-c32 (scholarly-interpretation): One proposed etymology of the name is Luwian pihassas 'lightning' and Pihassassi, a local Luwian-Hittite name in southern Cilicia for a weather deity associated with thunder and lightning.
pegasus-c33 (scholarly-interpretation): The Luwian interpretation was first suggested in 1952 and remains widely accepted, but Robin Lane Fox (2009) has criticized it as implausible.
pegasus-c34 (modern-popular-culture): During World War II the silhouette of Bellerophon mounted on the winged Pegasus was adopted in 1941 by the United Kingdom's newly raised parachute troops as their upper-sleeve insignia.
pegasus-c35 (historical-record): Pegasus has been a symbol of Tuscany since Benvenuto Cellini included it on a coin of 1537 honoring Cardinal Pietro Bembo.
