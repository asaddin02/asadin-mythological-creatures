isis-c01 (religious-tradition): Isis is an ancient Egyptian goddess.
isis-c02 (historical-record): Isis's name is first attested in the Fifth Dynasty Pyramid Texts.
isis-c03 (religious-tradition): Isis is the wife of Osiris and mother of Horus.
isis-c04 (religious-tradition): According to the myth, Isis revived Osiris after Seth killed him.
isis-c05 (religious-tradition): Isis nurtured Horus and protected him from Seth.
isis-c06 (historical-record): A statuette shows Isis nursing Horus.
isis-c07 (historical-record): Isis's cult began in the Nile Delta and spread throughout Egypt.
isis-c08 (historical-record): Statuettes of Isis and Horus emphasize her role as life-giver and protector.
isis-c09 (etymology): According to World History Encyclopedia, her name comes from the Egyptian Eset ("the seat"), referring to her stability and also the throne of Egypt, as she was considered the mother of every pharaoh through the king's association with Horus, her son.
isis-c10 (religious-tradition): Isis's symbols are the scorpion (who kept her safe in hiding), the kite (a kind of falcon whose shape she assumed in bringing her husband back to life), the empty throne, and the sistrum.
isis-c11 (religious-tradition): Isis was also known as Weret-Kekau ("the Great Magic") for her power and Mut-Netjer, "Mother of the Gods", and had many names depending on the role she was fulfilling.
isis-c12 (religious-tradition): In time Isis became so popular that all gods were considered mere aspects of her and she was the only Egyptian deity worshiped by everyone in the country.
isis-c13 (religious-tradition): Like the Eleusinian Mysteries, the cult of Isis grew into a mystery religion promising the secrets of life and death to initiates, who were sworn to secrecy.
isis-c14 (historical-record): Her temple on the island of Philae in Upper Egypt remained an active pilgrimage site for thousands of years until closed in the 6th century CE by the Christian emperor Justinian.
isis-c15 (historical-record): Isis eventually became associated with the sea and was protectress of sailors and merchants who wore talismans honoring her and invoked her aid in times of trouble.
isis-c16 (historical-record): Unlike other Egyptian gods, Isis transcended national borders and was worshiped by Greeks and Romans, who believed in her as the supreme deity who created the world.
isis-c17 (religious-tradition): Set was jealous of his brother's power and prestige and plotted to get rid of him, having a beautiful coffin made to Osiris's exact height.
isis-c18 (religious-tradition): Isis was distraught when her husband went missing and searched all through Egypt without success.
isis-c19 (religious-tradition): Isis cut Osiris from the tree and carried his body back to Egypt, hiding him from Set in the swampy region of the Nile Delta.
isis-c20 (religious-tradition): Isis was still able to revive Osiris and, in the form of a kite, flew around him and became pregnant with a son, Horus.
isis-c21 (religious-tradition): Though alive again, Osiris was incomplete and could no longer rule the living; he withdrew into the afterlife where he became Lord and Judge of the Dead.
isis-c22 (religious-tradition): Fearing what Set might do, Isis hid Horus in the swamps of Egypt until he was grown; Horus then emerged as a mighty warrior and battled Set for control of the world.
isis-c23 (religious-tradition): In the story of Isis and the Seven Scorpions, Isis called each scorpion by its secret name, dominating them, and recited spells of great magic; the poison left the child's body and he revived.
isis-c24 (religious-tradition): When Horus was an infant and Isis was hiding him in the swamp from Set, seven scorpions kept her company and formed a kind of bodyguard around her when she went out for food in the evenings.
isis-c25 (historical-record): When Alexander the Great conquered Egypt in 331 BCE his troops associated Isis with the Greek goddess Demeter.
isis-c26 (historical-record): Under Caligula (37-41 CE) the worship of Isis in Rome was firmly established by the emperor through the Isiac Festival in her honor.
isis-c27 (historical-record): A temple of Isis has been found in Pompeii, and another at the Roman site of Londinium (modern London) in Great Britain.
isis-c28 (scholarly-interpretation): According to World History Encyclopedia, her cult in Rome was the greatest rival of the young Christian religion, which drew on the image of Isis and the child-god Horus for the Madonna with the Christ child.
isis-c29 (historical-record): Philae continued as a vital site dedicated to Isis until the emperor Justinian closed it in 529 CE.
isis-c30 (scholarly-interpretation): Nephthys was never depicted as evil to Isis's good but was associated with death while Isis was associated with life, so the two sister-deities symbolized cosmic balance.
isis-c31 (historical-record): Isis, her husband, and her son replaced the Theban Triad of Amon, Mut, and Khons, the most popular trinity of gods in Egypt.
isis-c32 (religious-tradition): As the goddess who brought the yearly Nile inundation that fertilized the land she was Sati, and as the goddess who created and preserved life she was Ankhet.
isis-c33 (religious-tradition): Isis is regularly portrayed as a selfless, giving mother, wife, and protectress who puts others' interests and well-being ahead of her own.
isis-c34 (religious-tradition): Nut gave birth to five children by Geb: Osiris, Isis, Set, Nephthys, and Horus (known as Horus the Elder).
isis-c35 (religious-tradition): Osiris married Isis and, as the firstborn, ruled as Lord of the Earth with Isis as his queen and consort.
isis-c36 (religious-tradition): In Adlington's translation of Apuleius, the goddess calls herself the natural mother of all things, mistress of all the elements, the initial progeny of worlds, chief of divine powers, and Queen of heaven.
isis-c37 (religious-tradition): In Apuleius's work the goddess declares that at her will the planets of the air, the wholesome winds of the seas, and the silences of hell are disposed.
isis-c38 (religious-tradition): In Apuleius's work the goddess says she is worshipped under many names: the Phrygians call her mother of the Gods, the Athenians Minerva, the Cyprians Venus, and so on.
isis-c39 (religious-tradition): In Apuleius's work the Egyptians, excellent in all ancient doctrine and worshipping her with their own ceremonies, call her Queen Isis.
isis-c40 (religious-tradition): In the vision in Apuleius's work, the goddess holds a brass timbrel in her right hand and a golden cup in her left, from whose mouth a serpent, the asp, lifts its head.
isis-c41 (historical-record): Isis was a major goddess in ancient Egyptian religion whose worship spread throughout the Greco-Roman world.
isis-c42 (religious-tradition): Her reputed magical power was greater than that of all other gods, and she was said to govern the natural world and wield power over fate itself.
isis-c43 (religious-tradition): In one spell Isis creates a snake that bites Ra and makes him ill with its venom, then offers to cure him if he tells her his true, secret name.
isis-c44 (religious-tradition): In later texts Isis uses her powers of transformation to fight and destroy Set and his followers.
isis-c45 (religious-tradition): Isis was believed to help the dead enter the afterlife as she had helped Osiris, was considered the divine mother of the pharaoh, and her maternal aid was invoked in healing spells for ordinary people.
isis-c46 (historical-record): Neither Isis nor her husband Osiris were mentioned by name before the Fifth Dynasty (c. 2494-2345 BCE).
isis-c47 (historical-record): During the New Kingdom (c. 1550-1070 BCE) Isis took on traits that originally belonged to Hathor, the preeminent goddess of an earlier time.
isis-c48 (etymology): Isis's Egyptian name was written 𓊨𓏏𓐰𓆇𓁐 (ꜣst).
isis-c49 (scholarly-interpretation): The Egyptologist Kurt Sethe suggested she was originally a personification of thrones.
isis-c50 (scholarly-interpretation): Other scholars, such as Jürgen Osing and Klaus P. Kuhlmann, dispute this interpretation because of dissimilarities between Isis's name and the word for a throne, or a lack of evidence that the throne was ever deified.
isis-c51 (modern-popular-culture): Isis continues to appear in Western culture, particularly in esotericism and modern paganism, often as a personification of nature or the feminine aspect of divinity.
isis-c52 (historical-record): Isis's Greek devotees ascribed to her traits taken from Greek deities, such as the invention of marriage and the protection of ships at sea.
isis-c53 (scholarly-interpretation): According to Wikipedia, her worship may have influenced Christian beliefs and practices such as the veneration of Mary, but the evidence for this influence is ambiguous and often controversial.
isis-c54 (religious-tradition): Isis became the goddess of all the people of Egypt, male and female, royal and common alike.
