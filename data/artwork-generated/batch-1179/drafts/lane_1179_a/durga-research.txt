
=== durga (lengkap-informasi, batch-036)
Durga | tier rich | deity | jenis dewa | tradition-hindu

durga-c01 (religious-tradition): Durga is a principal goddess in Hinduism.
durga-c02 (etymology): Durga means inaccessible or impassable in Sanskrit.
durga-c03 (religious-tradition): Durga is a warrior manifestation of Devi who defeats demons.
durga-c04 (religious-tradition): Durga is depicted with ten arms and weapons, riding a lion or tiger.
durga-c05 (religious-tradition): Durga defeats Mahisha, a buffalo demon representing evil.
durga-c06 (religious-tradition): In the Devi Mahatmya, gods lend Durga weapons to defeat the demon.
durga-c07 (historical-record): Durga is worshipped during Navaratri in India and Nepal.
durga-c08 (historical-record): Dusshera is celebrated at the end of Navaratri.
durga-c09 (religious-tradition): The lion is Durga's divine mount in battle imagery against Mahisha.
durga-c10 (religious-tradition): One of Devi's most famous episodes is her slaying of Mahisa, a demon with a man's body and a buffalo's head, as told in the epic poem Candipat (Candi-mahatmya), part of the Skanda Purana.
durga-c11 (religious-tradition): In one version, the tremendous energy of the gods condensed into a single mass and formed Durga; the source notes this is only one version of her birth.
durga-c12 (religious-tradition): In other versions Devi has long existed as the daughter of Himavat, the personified deity of the Himalayas, and the angered gods only give her weapons: a discus, trident, bow, sword, dagger, harpoon, and noose.
durga-c13 (religious-tradition): Whenever Devi tried to deliver a fatal blow, Mahisa changed into another creature: a buffalo, a man, a lion, an elephant, and then a buffalo again.
durga-c14 (religious-tradition): The goddess pounced, straddled the creature and stabbed its neck with her trident; when Mahisa's spirit came out of the dying buffalo's mouth, Durga killed him by cutting off his head.
durga-c15 (historical-record): Durga slaying Mahisa is a popular subject in Hindu art; one of the earliest representations is in a cave temple near Mallapuram (7th or 8th century CE) and on the Kailasanatha temple at Ellora (mid-8th century CE).
durga-c16 (religious-tradition): Devi's character has two opposing sides: Uma, the benevolent, and Durga, the terrible; it is the fiercer side that is most frequently worshipped.
durga-c17 (religious-tradition): Mahisa revealed himself and boasted of his wealth and power so that Durga would marry him; Durga responded dismissively to the proposal by transforming herself into fire.
durga-c18 (scholarly-interpretation): Durga is believed to have originated as an ancient goddess worshipped by indigenous mountain-dwellers of the Indian subcontinent, before being established in the main Hindu pantheon by the 4th century CE.
durga-c19 (religious-tradition): She is best known as Mahishasura-mardini for slaying the buffalo demon Mahishasura; in her battles with Shumbha and Nishumbha she manifests other warrior goddesses, the Matrikas and Kali, to aid her.
durga-c20 (religious-tradition): She is associated with protection, strength, motherhood, destruction and war; her legends center on combating evil and demonic forces that threaten peace, dharma and cosmic order, representing the power of good over evil.
durga-c21 (religious-tradition): Durga traditionally holds the weapons of various male gods, given to her to fight evil because they regard her as shakti (energy, power); these include the discus, conch, bow, arrow, sword, javelin, trident, shield, mace, pink lotus and noose.
durga-c22 (etymology): The word Durga literally means "impassable", "invincible, unassailable"; it is related to durg, meaning "fortress, something difficult to defeat or pass".
durga-c23 (religious-tradition): In Vaishnava contexts, Durga is revered as Yoganidra, the divine slumber, younger sister and a female version of Vishnu.
durga-c24 (religious-tradition): In Vaishnava tradition, Vishnu gives Durga the task of moving Devaki's seventh child into Rohini's womb and of being born as the infant daughter of Yashoda and Nanda so she could be swapped with Krishna.
durga-c25 (religious-tradition): In Shaktism Durga has many epithets, and her nine appellations (Navadurga) are Shailaputri, Brahmacharini, Chandraghanta, Kushmanda, Skandamata, Katyayini, Kalaratri, Mahagauri and Siddhidatri.
durga-c26 (historical-record): On the north wall of a granite cave in Mamallapuram, Tamil Nadu, there is a large relief of Durga slaying Mahisasura, carved around 630-674 CE.
durga-c27 (historical-record): Sixth-century CE inscriptions in early Siddhamatrika script, such as at the Nagarjuni hill cave in the Maukhari era, already mention the legend of her victory over Mahishasura.
durga-c28 (scholarly-interpretation): Durga as a demon-slaying goddess was likely well established when the classic text Devi Mahatmya was composed, which scholars variously date to between 400 and 600 CE.
durga-c29 (religious-tradition): Her iconography typically shows her riding a lion or a tiger, with between eight and eighteen hands, each holding a weapon.
durga-c30 (religious-tradition): Durga iconography has been flexible in Hindu traditions; for example some intellectuals place a pen or other writing implement in her hand because they consider their stylus their weapon.
durga-c31 (religious-tradition): In this account Himavan, the personification of the Himalayas, gifted a lion as her mount.
durga-c32 (historical-record): Durga has a significant following all over Nepal, India, Bangladesh and many other countries.
durga-c33 (scholarly-interpretation): Durga is typically portrayed as a celibate warrior goddess, but at a certain point in her history she becomes associated with the god Shiva as his wife.
durga-c34 (scholarly-interpretation): Durga is a very popular deity; the name is applied indifferently to all goddesses with a terrible appearance and in a fighting attitude.
durga-c35 (religious-tradition): The Saivagama specifically describes nine varieties of Durga, all with two arms but different weapons.
durga-c36 (scholarly-interpretation): In this role as Shiva's wife, Durga assumes domestic characteristics and is often identified with the goddess Parvati.
durga-c37 (religious-tradition): The general description of Durga in the Kasyapa-Silpa shows her with four arms and two eyes.
durga-c38 (historical-record): A figure of Durga from Mahabalipuram stands on the buffalo's head and has eight arms; the discus and the conch are in the uppermost ones.
durga-c39 (religious-tradition): Durga is commonly known as Mahishasura-mardini for slaying the half-buffalo demon Mahishasura, and also as Vindhyavasini (she who dwells in the Vindhya Mountains).
durga-c40 (religious-tradition): Durga is also known as Durgati Nashini, meaning one who eliminates suffering.
durga-c41 (religious-tradition): Mahisa managed to drive most of the gods out of heaven, forcing them to wander the earth as mere men.
