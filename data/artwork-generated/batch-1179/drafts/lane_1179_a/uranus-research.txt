
=== uranus (lengkap-informasi, batch-034)
Uranus | tier rich | deity | jenis dewa | greek-mythology
Ringkas: Uranus or Ouranos is the primordial god and personification of the sky in Greek mythology. In Hesiod he fathers the Titans, Cyclopes and Hecatoncheires until his son Cronus castrates him.
Kemampuan: prophecy — Ouranos prophesied the fall of the Titans, and with Gaia warned Cronus and Zeus that their sons would overthrow them. [uranus-c14, uranus-c22, uranus-c24]
uranus-c01 (traditional-belief): Uranus or Ouranos is the personification of the sky in Greek mythology.
uranus-c02 (traditional-belief): Uranus is a primordial sky god.
uranus-c03 (traditional-belief): Gaia gave birth to Uranus and chose him as her equal.
uranus-c04 (traditional-belief): Gaia and Uranus parented the Titans, Cyclopes, and Hecatoncheires.
uranus-c05 (traditional-belief): Uranus hid his children within the earth.
uranus-c06 (traditional-belief): Gaia gave Cronus a sickle to confront Uranus.
uranus-c07 (traditional-belief): Cronus castrated Uranus with the sickle.
uranus-c08 (traditional-belief): Uranus's blood falling on Gaia led to the birth of Erinyes, Giants, and Meliae.
uranus-c09 (traditional-belief): In this account, Aphrodite was born from sea foam.
uranus-c10 (scholarly-interpretation): Caelus is Uranus's Roman counterpart.
uranus-c11 (historical-record): The Greeks imagined the sky as a solid brass dome decorated with stars, its edges resting on the outermost limits of the flat earth; Ouranos was the literal sky, as his consort Gaia was the earth.
uranus-c12 (traditional-belief): Ouranos and Gaia had twelve sons and six daughters; he locked the eldest, the giant Cyclopes and Hecatoncheires, away inside the belly of Earth.
uranus-c13 (traditional-belief): In this account four of the children took positions at the corners of the world to seize their father while Kronos, in the centre, castrated Ouranos with an adamantine sickle.
uranus-c14 (traditional-belief): Ouranos prophesied the fall of the Titans and their punishment for their crimes, a prophecy fulfilled when Zeus deposed the five brothers and cast them into the pit of Tartaros.
uranus-c15 (scholarly-interpretation): Ouranos does not appear in early Greek art, but Egyptian depictions of the sky-goddess Nut show how he was imagined: a gigantic, star-spangled man with long limbs, resting on all fours.
uranus-c16 (historical-record): In the Roman era he was often depicted as Aion, god of eternal time, as a man holding the zodiac-wheel and standing above the reclining Gaia.
uranus-c17 (historical-record): According to the writer of the War of the Titans, Ouranos was the son of Aither.
uranus-c18 (religious-tradition): In Orphism, Phanes gave birth to Nyx (Night), and then Nyx bore Gaia and Uranus with Phanes.
uranus-c19 (historical-record): Although there is barely any surviving evidence of worship of Uranus, one Orphic hymn is dedicated to him.
uranus-c20 (traditional-belief): Uranus, along with Gaia and Styx, was often the witness to the oaths of the gods.
uranus-c21 (traditional-belief): According to the Theogony, Tartarus, Earth and the heavens are equally far apart; an anvil falling from Uranus would take nine days and nights to reach Gaia.
uranus-c22 (traditional-belief): Uranus lost power to Cronus but warned him that he too would be overthrown by his son.
uranus-c23 (traditional-belief): A desperate Rhea asked Uranus and Gaia for help, and they helped her flee to Crete, where Zeus was born.
uranus-c24 (traditional-belief): Uranus and Gaia told Zeus that Metis would have a strong, wise daughter and a son who would overthrow his father to become king of gods and men.
uranus-c25 (scholarly-interpretation): According to World History Encyclopedia, the myth closely follows the Uranus-Cronus-Zeus succession myth in Hesiod's Theogony, and Anu, king of Heaven, can clearly be identified as Uranus.
uranus-c26 (historical-record): Tablets found in excavations at the village of Boĝazköy recount the Hittite story of the storm god Teššub's rise to power and the attempts to overthrow him.
uranus-c27 (historical-record): What is considered a Phoenician version of the succession myth is recorded by Herennius Philo (Philo of Byblos, 1st or 2nd century CE).
uranus-c28 (historical-record): In this succession version, Epigeios, later named Uranus, replaced his father as king and married his sister Ge (Gaia).
uranus-c29 (historical-record): In that version, Cronus emasculated Uranus in the 32nd year of his reign.
uranus-c30 (historical-record): The astronomer Johann Elert Bode suggested that the planet be called Uranus in honour of the primordial deity.
uranus-c31 (historical-record): The planet Uranus was discovered in 1781 by the British-German astronomer William Herschel.
uranus-c32 (historical-record): In his Theogony, Hesiod (700 BCE) writes that Gaia bore Uranus (Heaven) along with Pontus (the Sea) and Ourea (the Mountains).
uranus-c33 (historical-record): Orphism was a religious movement that rose to prominence during the 6th century BCE.
uranus-c34 (traditional-belief): According to Hesiod, Earth first bore starry Heaven, equal to herself, to cover her on every side and be an ever-sure abiding place for the blessed gods.
uranus-c35 (traditional-belief): Hesiod writes that Earth afterwards lay with Heaven and bore Oceanus, Coeus, Crius, Hyperion, Iapetus, Theia, Rhea, Themis, Mnemosyne, Phoebe and Tethys.
uranus-c36 (traditional-belief): According to Hesiod, the most terrible children of Earth and Heaven were hated by their father from the first; as each was born he hid it in a secret place of Earth and would not let it come up into the light, and Heaven rejoiced in his evil doing.
uranus-c37 (traditional-belief): According to Hesiod, Earth made the element of grey flint into a great sickle and told her plan to her sons.
uranus-c38 (traditional-belief): In Hesiod, Heaven came bringing night and longing for love and spread himself upon Earth; his son from his ambush lopped off his father's members with the long jagged sickle and cast them behind him.
uranus-c39 (traditional-belief): According to Hesiod, Earth received all the bloody drops and as the seasons turned bore the strong Erinyes, the Giants in gleaming armour with long spears, and the Nymphs called Meliae.
uranus-c40 (traditional-belief): According to Hesiod, great Heaven called his sons Titans (Strainers) in reproach, saying they strained and did presumptuously a fearful deed, and that vengeance for it would come afterwards.
uranus-c41 (historical-record): No cult addressed directly to Uranus survived into classical times, and Uranus is not among the usual themes of Greek painted pottery.
uranus-c42 (traditional-belief): Uranus is the personification of the sky and one of the Greek primordial deities; according to Hesiod he was the son and husband of Gaia, with whom he fathered the first generation of Titans.
uranus-c43 (etymology): Most linguists trace the etymology of the name Οὐρανός to a Proto-Greek form *Worsanós.
uranus-c44 (etymology): The basic Indo-European root makes Ouranos the "rain-maker" or the "lord of rain".
uranus-c45 (scholarly-interpretation): Georges Dumézil (1934) identified Uranus with the Vedic deity Váruṇa, but the etymological equation is now considered untenable.
uranus-c46 (historical-record): Uranus is identified on the Gigantomachy frieze of the Pergamon Altar, bearded and winged, fighting the Giants with a sword.
uranus-c47 (scholarly-interpretation): After his castration, Uranus recedes into the background.
