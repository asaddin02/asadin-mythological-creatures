parvati-c01 (religious-tradition): Parvati is a Hindu goddess whose name means daughter of the mountain and who is also called Uma.
parvati-c02 (religious-tradition): Parvati is Shiva's wife and the mother of Ganesha and Skanda, associated with Mahadevi's benign aspect.
parvati-c03 (religious-tradition): Purana stories recount Parvati's rebirth as daughter of Himavat and Mena, her ascetic practice, and marriage to Shiva.
parvati-c04 (religious-tradition): Parvati is also Shiva's shakti, mother of the universe, and an ascetic in her own right.
parvati-c05 (religious-tradition): The Smithsonian describes Parvati or Uma as a mountain goddess and divine feminine power.
parvati-c06 (religious-tradition): The Puranas tell of Parvati's wish to marry Shiva.
parvati-c07 (religious-tradition): Parvati performed severe asceticism to gain power and win over the meditating Shiva.
parvati-c08 (historical-record): Parvati is worshiped as Shiva's wife and as an independent goddess.
parvati-c09 (historical-record): The Asia Society's Parvati statue stands in the triple-bend tribhanga pose.
parvati-c10 (historical-record): In South India, Parvati is also known as Uma.
parvati-c11 (traditional-belief): In Kalidasa's poem, Parvati is described as born again, having been famed in an earlier life as Shiva's faithful wife.
parvati-c12 (traditional-belief): In her earlier life her proud father dared to scorn the god, and she, defending the lord she loved, left her mortal body.
parvati-c13 (traditional-belief): At first her only name was "Child of the Mountain"; when her anxious mother cried "Forbear!" at her stern penance, the warning became a new title.
parvati-c14 (traditional-belief): In the poem, the flashes of Shiva's angry eye scorch the King of Love to ashes.
parvati-c15 (traditional-belief): In the poem, only penance can help her win the cold heart of the stern deity.
parvati-c16 (traditional-belief): Parvati would not spare her fair, frail body if sterner penance might prevail.
parvati-c17 (traditional-belief): Shiva tells her that, won by her penance and holy vows, he bows before her as her willing slave.
parvati-c18 (traditional-belief): According to Wikipedia, Sati is reborn as Parvati, daughter of Himavat and Mainavati, and named Parvati, "she from the mountains", after her father.
parvati-c19 (traditional-belief): In the story Indra sends Kama, the god of desire and love, to awaken Shiva from meditation.
parvati-c20 (traditional-belief): After the marriage Parvati moves to Mount Kailash, Shiva's residence; to them are born Kartikeya (Skanda, Murugan), leader of celestial armies, and Ganesha, god of wisdom who removes obstacles.
parvati-c21 (traditional-belief): In one story Parvati shaped a boy from turmeric paste used to cleanse her body and gave it life, and thus Ganesha was born.
parvati-c22 (traditional-belief): In that story Shiva, infuriated, severed the boy's head with his trident.
parvati-c23 (traditional-belief): At Parvati's demand Shiva restored the boy by attaching an elephant's head, giving rise to the elephant-headed deity.
parvati-c24 (scholarly-interpretation): Parvati, the gentle aspect of Devi Shakti, is usually represented as fair, beautiful, and benevolent.
parvati-c25 (scholarly-interpretation): Parvati's hands may hold a trident, mirror, rosary, bell, dish, goad, sugarcane stalk, or flowers such as a lotus.
parvati-c26 (religious-tradition): In the Skanda Purana Parvati takes the form of a warrior-goddess and defeats a demon named Durg who takes the form of a buffalo; in this aspect she is known as Durga.
parvati-c27 (religious-tradition): In the Linga Purana, at Shiva's request, Parvati transforms into Kali to destroy the asura (demon) Daruk.
parvati-c28 (religious-tradition): As Mahakali she identifies and destroys evil for protection, and as Annapurna she creates food and abundance for nourishment.
parvati-c29 (traditional-belief): In the story of the ten Mahavidyas, Parvati's rage at Shiva's attempt to leave takes the form of ten terrifying goddesses who block his every exit.
parvati-c30 (scholarly-interpretation): Shaiva approaches tend to view Parvati as Shiva's submissive, obedient wife, whereas Shaktas stress her equality or even superiority to her consort.
parvati-c31 (scholarly-interpretation): Three images are central to Parvati's ethos, iconography, and philosophy: Shiva-Shakti, Shiva as Ardhanarishvara (the Lord who is half-woman), and the linga and the yoni.
parvati-c32 (traditional-belief): Teej is a significant festival for Hindu women, particularly in the northern and western states of India, and Parvati is its primary deity.
parvati-c33 (traditional-belief): In Bali, Parvati is spelled Parwati and is a principal goddess of Balinese Hinduism; she is more often called Uma and sometimes Giriputri (daughter of the mountains).
parvati-c34 (traditional-belief): As Dewi Danu she presides over waters, Lake Batur, and Gunung Batur, a major volcano in Bali.
parvati-c35 (historical-record): Sati-Parvati appears in the epic period (400 BCE-400 CE), when both the Ramayana and the Mahabharata present Parvati as Shiva's wife.
parvati-c36 (etymology): Parvata is one of the Sanskrit words for "mountain"; the name Parvati derives from her incarnation as daughter of king Himavan (also called Himavata, Parvata).
parvati-c37 (religious-tradition): Parvati (Sanskrit: पार्वती), also known as Uma and Gauri, is one of the principal goddesses in Hinduism.
parvati-c38 (traditional-belief): Meenakshi is a regional form of Parvati in Madurai and is considered the queen of Madurai.
parvati-c39 (scholarly-interpretation): Kalidasa's epic Kumarasambhavam ("Birth of Kumara") tells of the maiden Parvati, determined to marry Shiva and draw him out of his austere, aloof world.
parvati-c40 (historical-record): Only with Kalidasa's plays (5th-6th centuries) and the Puranas (4th through 13th centuries) do the stories of Sati-Parvati and Shiva gain fuller detail.
