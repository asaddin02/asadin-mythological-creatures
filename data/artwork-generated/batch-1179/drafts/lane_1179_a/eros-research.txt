
=== eros (lengkap-informasi, batch-033)
Eros | tier rich | deity | jenis dewa | greek-mythology
Ringkas: Eros is the Greek god of love and desire, portrayed either as a primordial god of creation or as Aphrodite's son armed with bow and arrows.
Kemampuan: mind-manipulation — Eros's arrows make humans, heroes, and gods fall in love; he is described as overcoming the mind and wise counsels of all gods and all men. [eros-c05, eros-c31]
Kemampuan: null — In one tale Eros has a gold-tipped arrow that rouses love and a lead-tipped arrow that routs it. [eros-c36]
Kemampuan: null — Eros is said to tame lions and tigers, break Zeus's thunderbolts, and deprive Heracles of his arms. [eros-c40]
Kemampuan: shapeshifting — In Hyginus's tale, Eros and Aphrodite changed their forms into fishes to escape danger. [eros-c44]

eros-c01 (religious-tradition): Eros is the Greek god of love and passionate desire.
eros-c02 (traditional-belief): One tradition describes Eros as a primordial god emerging at creation.
eros-c03 (traditional-belief): In another tradition, the younger Eros is Aphrodite's son carrying bow and arrows.
eros-c04 (traditional-belief): One genealogy makes Eros the son of Aphrodite and Ares.
eros-c05 (traditional-belief): Eros's arrows were said to make humans, heroes, and gods fall in love.
eros-c06 (traditional-belief): Eros fell in love with and married Psyche despite Aphrodite's opposition.
eros-c07 (historical-record): Eros received cult worship at Thespiae, Athens, Megara, and other places.
eros-c08 (historical-record): Classical art usually portrays Eros as a winged youth.
eros-c09 (historical-record): In the Hellenistic period, depiction of Eros as a baby became common.
eros-c10 (scholarly-interpretation): Cupid is the Roman counterpart of Eros.
eros-c11 (traditional-belief): Eros picks his targets without warning and strikes their hearts, causing confusion and overwhelming feelings.
eros-c12 (historical-record): In Greek art Eros is most often shown as a carefree, beautiful youth crowned with flowers, especially roses, which were closely associated with him.
eros-c13 (traditional-belief): According to Hesiod's Theogony, Eros was one of the primeval gods who, with Chaos and Gaia (Earth), was responsible for the Creation.
eros-c14 (traditional-belief): In other traditions such as the Orphic cosmogonies, Eros is born a hermaphrodite from an egg put by the Titan Chronos (Time) into the womb of Chaos.
eros-c15 (traditional-belief): The comic playwright Aristophanes (c. 460-c. 380 BCE) uses the same idea but has Eros hatch from a silver egg laid by Nyx (Night) and Aither (Wind).
eros-c16 (traditional-belief): In some traditions Eros had a younger brother, Anteros, a much darker figure and an avenger of unrequited love.
eros-c17 (traditional-belief): In Greek myth Eros was associated above all with fertility, desire and sexual love, and because such passions were hard to control he was considered something of a cunning trickster.
eros-c18 (traditional-belief): Sometimes the god is playful and harmlessly mischievous, but at other times cruel with surprise attacks that bring only reckless passion and confusion.
eros-c19 (scholarly-interpretation): According to the source, Eros's lack of discipline and general unreliability may explain why he was never counted among the 12 Olympian gods.
eros-c20 (traditional-belief): Eros was also considered the specific protector of homosexual love.
eros-c21 (traditional-belief): In one famous episode Apollo ridiculed Eros's skill as an archer, and Eros shot one of his arrows at the great god, making him fall in love with the nymph Daphne.
eros-c22 (traditional-belief): Another instance: Eros made Medea fall in love with the great hero Jason, who seized the Golden Fleece.
eros-c23 (historical-record): Altars to Eros were placed at the Academy of Athens and at the gymnasium at Elis.
eros-c24 (historical-record): In ancient Greek art from the 6th century BCE Eros is usually depicted as a winged adolescent who often carries a victory wreath.
eros-c25 (historical-record): Eros is shown carrying a bow with any frequency only from the 4th century BCE, though the first literary reference is much earlier, in Euripides' Iphigenia in Aulis (c. 406 BCE).
eros-c26 (historical-record): Figures of Eros can also appear in twos or threes, called Erotes, symbolizing the different forms love can take.
eros-c27 (historical-record): Only in later Roman art is Eros, under his new name Cupid, commonly portrayed as a chubby, mischievous baby.
eros-c28 (traditional-belief): In Plato's Symposium a speaker calls Love the most ancient of the gods, the most honoured, and the most powerful in helping men gain virtue and blessedness.
eros-c29 (historical-record): Eros is also present on the east frieze of the Parthenon, shown as a child next to Aphrodite.
eros-c30 (traditional-belief): Eros was the driving force behind the generation of new life in the cosmos; the Orphics named him Phanes, a primal being hatched from the world-egg.
eros-c31 (traditional-belief): In Hesiod's Theogony Eros is fairest among the deathless gods, who unnerves the limbs and overcomes the mind and wise counsels of all gods and all men.
eros-c32 (traditional-belief): In Aristophanes' Birds black-winged Nyx lays an egg in the deeps of Erebos, and after long ages the graceful Eros springs from it with glittering golden wings.
eros-c33 (etymology): Theoi translates the name Eros as "sexual desire".
eros-c34 (traditional-belief): It was he who lit the flame of love in the hearts of gods and men, armed with either a bow and arrows or a flaming torch.
eros-c35 (traditional-belief): Eros was often portrayed as the disobedient but fiercely loyal child of Aphrodite.
eros-c36 (traditional-belief): In Ovid's Metamorphoses Eros has two opposing arrows: one rouses love with a piercing gold point, the other routs it with a blunt tip of lead.
eros-c37 (traditional-belief): According to Pausanias, most men consider Eros the youngest of the gods and the son of Aphrodite.
eros-c38 (traditional-belief): According to a scholion, Alcaeus said Eros was the child of Iris and Zephyros, while Sappho made him the child of Aphrodite and Ouranos.
eros-c39 (traditional-belief): In the Symposium Eros is conceived (by Penia) and, being a lover of beauty and born on Aphrodite's birthday, becomes her follower and attendant.
eros-c40 (scholarly-interpretation): According to the Dictionary of Greek and Roman Biography and Mythology, Eros tames lions and tigers, breaks Zeus's thunderbolts, deprives Heracles of his arms, and sports with sea monsters.
eros-c41 (scholarly-interpretation): Things sacred to Eros that often accompany him in art include the rose, wild beasts he tames, the hare, the cock, and the ram.
eros-c42 (scholarly-interpretation): Eros was a favourite subject of ancient sculptors, and his image seems to have been perfected by Praxiteles, who conceived him as a full-grown youth of perfect beauty.
eros-c43 (scholarly-interpretation): Homer does not mention Eros; Hesiod, the earliest author to mention him, describes him as the cosmogonic Eros.
eros-c44 (traditional-belief): In Hyginus's tale, when Typhon appeared at the Euphrates, Venus (Aphrodite) and her son Cupid (Eros) threw themselves into the river and changed into fishes, escaping danger.
eros-c45 (traditional-belief): In Ovid's Metamorphoses, as Eros kissed his mother, an arrow from his quiver unknowingly grazed Aphrodite's breast.
eros-c46 (traditional-belief): In Hesiod's Theogony Eros and comely Himeros (Desire) followed Aphrodite at her birth and as she went into the assembly of the gods.
eros-c47 (traditional-belief): In Nonnus's Dionysiaca, when Typhoeus besieged Olympos, bold Eros fled in panic, leaving his arrows behind.
eros-c48 (traditional-belief): In Apollonius's Argonautica, Athena says that, sprung from Zeus, she has never felt the arrows of the Boy (Eros).
eros-c49 (traditional-belief): Theoi lists Hedone as Eros's offspring by Psyche, citing Apuleius.
eros-c50 (historical-record): The scene of Eros and Himeros accompanying Aphrodite was particularly popular in ancient art, the godlings fluttering about the goddess reclining in a conch-shell.
