
=== odin (lengkap-informasi, batch-030)
Odin | tier rich | deity | jenis dewa | norse-mythology
Ringkas: Odin is the main god of Norse mythology, depicted as wise and one-eyed and associated with war, poetry, the dead, runes, and magic.
Kemampuan: magic — Odin is called the god of magic, and his magic often leads him to be seen as a shaman. [odin-c02, odin-c28]
Kemampuan: prophecy — Odin mainly used magic to catch glimpses of the future, and is described as falling into trances and having visions. [odin-c32, odin-c20]
Kemampuan: healing — Odin has a function as a god of healing. [odin-c28]
Kemampuan: shapeshifting — In the story of the mead of poetry, Odin shape-shifts into an eagle to escape. [odin-c33]
Kemampuan: null — In southern and western Germanic sources, Odin decides whether battles or individual warriors are victorious. [odin-c19]
Kemampuan: null — The spear Gungnir is a main attribute, and Odin advances with it against the Fenris-wolf at Ragnarök. The ring Draupnir drips eight new rings every nine nights. [odin-c09, odin-c25, odin-c10]
Kemampuan: null — Odin's knowledge is said to come from Mímir's head or from drinking from Mímir's well, and according to the Prose Edda he left one eye as a pledge. [odin-c11, odin-c12]
odin-c01 (historical-record): Odin is a leading Norse god depicted as wise and one-eyed.
odin-c02 (religious-tradition): Odin is associated with poetry, the dead, runes, and magic.
odin-c03 (religious-tradition): Odin's two ravens are Huginn and Muninn.
odin-c04 (religious-tradition): Odin is killed by Fenrir at Ragnarök.
odin-c05 (historical-record): Odin's tales survive in Edda writings and Icelandic sagas recorded in the Middle Ages.
odin-c06 (religious-tradition): Odin gathers fallen warriors in Valhalla.
odin-c07 (religious-tradition): The ravens fly to gather news for Odin.
odin-c08 (religious-tradition): Odin is recognised by his hat and cloak, long beard, and only one eye.
odin-c09 (religious-tradition): His spear Gungnir is one of his main attributes.
odin-c10 (religious-tradition): Other attributes of Odin are the ring Draupnir, which drips eight new rings every nine nights, and the eight-legged horse Sleipnir.
odin-c11 (religious-tradition): Odin's immense knowledge is said to come from Mímir's head, or else from drinking from Mímir's well.
odin-c12 (historical-record): According to the Prose Edda, Alfather received a drink from Mimer's well only after leaving one of his eyes there as a pledge.
odin-c13 (religious-tradition): Odin hangs for nine nights from a branch of Yggdrasil, the World Tree, with a spear wound that brings him close to death, and only then are the runes revealed to him.
odin-c14 (religious-tradition): Snorri explains that Odin's poetic skill comes from stealing the mead of the skalds (poets) after sleeping for three nights with the giantess Gunnlǫð, the mead's guardian.
odin-c15 (religious-tradition): Borr and the giantess Bestla sired Odin and his brothers, usually named Vili and Vé.
odin-c16 (religious-tradition): Odin and his brothers slew the proto-giant Ymir and made the earth from his flesh, the sky from his skull, the mountains from his bones, and the sea from his blood.
odin-c17 (religious-tradition): Odin's link with war extends to a link with the dead, shown in his role as leader of the Wild Hunt, an army of the dead who ride on the storms.
odin-c18 (religious-tradition): Odin's Valkyries, supernatural warrior women, bring fighters slain in battle to Valhalla; these fighters, the Einherjar, become Odin's strike-force against the powers of the Underworld at Ragnarök.
odin-c19 (religious-tradition): In southern and western Germanic sources, Odin decided whether battles or individual warriors would be victorious.
odin-c20 (scholarly-interpretation): Odin is described as falling into trances, leaving his body to travel in the spirit-form of an animal, and several times having visions of wisdom provoked by ordeals.
odin-c21 (historical-record): In 1070 CE Adam of Bremen wrote that at the Old Uppsala temple a statue of Thor stood in the centre with Odin and Freyr at the sides, and that sacrifices were made to Odin in times of war.
odin-c22 (etymology): Several of Odin's names and titles stem from the reconstructed Proto-Germanic theonym Wōðanaz, meaning "lord of frenzy" or "leader of the possessed".
odin-c23 (historical-record): Odin also exists in Germanic mythology as Woden (Old English), Wodan (Old Franconian), and Wutan or Wuotan (Old High German).
odin-c24 (historical-record): According to the Prose Edda, the highest god is called Alfather, and in old Asgard he had twelve names.
odin-c25 (historical-record): In the Prose Edda, Odin rides first, with golden helmet, resplendent byrnie, and his spear Gungner, and advances against the Fenris-wolf.
odin-c26 (modern-popular-culture): Odin was adapted in Neil Gaiman's 2001 novel American Gods as Mr. Wednesday, a con artist revealed to be an incarnation of Odin.
odin-c27 (modern-popular-culture): Odin was adapted as a character by Marvel Comics, first appearing in the Journey into Mystery series in 1962.
odin-c28 (scholarly-interpretation): The nature of Odin's magic often leads him to be seen as a shaman, an aspect reinforced by his function as a god of healing.
odin-c29 (religious-tradition): Wider sources depict Odin as son of Bestla and Borr, brother of Vili and Vé, and husband of the goddess Frigg, with whom he fathered Baldr; he also fathered Thor with the earth-goddess Jörð.
odin-c30 (scholarly-interpretation): According to World History Encyclopedia, Odin's role as Allfather is more a literary theme from later sources, seemingly influenced by Christian names for God, than a reflection of his status in Viking Age societies.
odin-c31 (historical-record): According to the Prose Edda, Odin is called Alfather because he is the father of all gods and men and of all things made by him and his might.
odin-c32 (scholarly-interpretation): Odin mainly used magic to catch glimpses of the future.
odin-c33 (religious-tradition): In the story of the mead of poetry, Odin is allowed three sips but drains the containers, shape-shifts into an eagle and flees.
odin-c34 (religious-tradition): Odin resides in Asgard, the stronghold and home of the gods.
odin-c35 (historical-record): Bronze Age Swedish rock carvings show figures of a god holding a spear who might be linked to Odin, and by at least 500 CE he clearly appears on ornaments alongside birds and warriors.
odin-c36 (historical-record): Odin's main group of worshippers mostly consisted of the elite, namely kings, chieftains and poets, but also included warriors.
odin-c37 (scholarly-interpretation): Skaldic poetry names Baldr, Thor and Vali as Odin's sons, whereas the 13th-century CE author Snorri Sturluson adds Heimdall, Týr, Bragi, Vidarr and Hodr.
odin-c38 (religious-tradition): In Vafþrúðnismál Odin starts a battle of wits with the giant Vafþrúðnir and beats him; in Grímnismál a disguised Odin is tortured and ends up revealing his mythical knowledge to King Geirroðr.
odin-c39 (etymology): The modern English weekday name Wednesday traces its origin to "day of Woden" (wōdnesdæg in Old English), still seen in the Dutch name woensdag.
