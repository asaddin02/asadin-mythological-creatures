hermes-c01 (religious-tradition): Hermes is a Greek Olympian herald of the gods and protector of travelers and merchants.
hermes-c02 (religious-tradition): Hermes serves as a guide of souls to the afterlife.
hermes-c03 (religious-tradition): Theoi identifies Hermes with flocks, travelers, roads, trade, cunning, heralds, and diplomacy.
hermes-c04 (historical-record): Hermes is shown as a beardless youth or older bearded man with winged boots and a herald's wand.
hermes-c05 (religious-tradition): World History Encyclopedia calls Hermes the Olympian gods' herald and messenger.
hermes-c06 (historical-record): Romans knew Hermes as Mercury.
hermes-c07 (scholarly-interpretation): According to World History Encyclopedia, in his position as herald he came to symbolise the crossing of boundaries as a guide between the realms of gods and humanity.
hermes-c08 (traditional-belief): Hermes is said to have invented the lyre, the alphabet, and dice; the latter explains why gamblers loved him.
hermes-c09 (traditional-belief): Hermes was the patron of shepherds and invented the panpipes they used to call their flock.
hermes-c10 (traditional-belief): With origins as an Arcadian fertility god with a special love for the Peloponnese, the ancient Greeks believed Hermes was the son of Zeus and the nymph Maia (daughter of the Titan Atlas) and was born on Mt. Cyllene in Arcadia.
hermes-c11 (traditional-belief): While still a baby, Hermes stole his half-brother Apollo's herd of 50 sacred cattle from Pieria, cleverly reversing their hoofmarks with bark shoes to make them hard to track.
hermes-c12 (traditional-belief): After a hearing before Zeus and the Olympian gods, Hermes was permitted to keep the herd (now 48, since he had sacrificed two) if he gave Apollo his lyre.
hermes-c13 (traditional-belief): Perhaps most celebrated was his killing of the many-eyed (some accounts say 100-eyed) monster Argos on Zeus's orders to free Io.
hermes-c14 (traditional-belief): One of his most famous regular roles was leading souls to the river Styx in the underworld, where the boatman Charon would take them to Hades.
hermes-c15 (traditional-belief): Another hero helped by Hermes was Perseus: Hermes gave him an unbreakable sword or sickle (harpe) of adamantine and guided him to the three Graeae who would reveal Medusa's location.
hermes-c16 (traditional-belief): Hermes gives particular help to Odysseus, especially on his long voyage home to Ithaca (as told in Homer's Odyssey), for example by giving him an antidote to Circe's spells.
hermes-c17 (historical-record): Hermes was the patron of travellers, and stone pillars (hermae) with a phallus symbol were often set up along roadsides as guides and to offer good fortune to passers-by.
hermes-c18 (historical-record): In Archaic and Classical Greek art Hermes is shown holding the kerykeion or caduceus staff (signifying his role as herald), wearing winged sandals (symbolic of his role as messenger), a long tunic or leopard skin, sometimes a winged cap (petasos), and occasionally a lyre.
hermes-c19 (historical-record): Hermes has a very long history, being mentioned in the Linear B tablets of the Mycenaean civilization, at its height from the 15th to 13th century BCE.
hermes-c20 (etymology): Orators regarded the god who transferred words from sender to receiver as their patron, as did interpreters, and even today the study and interpretation of texts carries his name: hermeneutics.
hermes-c21 (traditional-belief): Hermes was also known as something of a trickster, at one time or another stealing Poseidon's trident, Artemis' arrows, and Aphrodite's girdle.
hermes-c22 (historical-record): Athens had one of the oldest cults of the god, where the Hermaia festival for young boys was held annually.
hermes-c23 (traditional-belief): Hermes also freed Ares from his year-long imprisonment in a cauldron by the twin Giants Otus and Ephialtes.
hermes-c24 (traditional-belief): According to Theoi, as a newborn Hermes snuck out of his crib, stole Apollon's cattle, and crafted the first lyre from a tortoise-shell; Zeus was so amused that he granted him a place among the twelve supreme gods of Olympos.
hermes-c25 (traditional-belief): Hermes turned the tell-tale herdsman Battos into stone as punishment for reporting his theft of Apollon's cattle.
hermes-c26 (historical-record): Hermes' most distinctive attribute was the herald's wand (Greek kerykeion, Latin caduceus), though he was sometimes armed with a short sword instead.
hermes-c27 (historical-record): Hermes' sacred animals were the ram and the hare; in ancient art he rode on the back of a large ram.
hermes-c28 (traditional-belief): Homeric Hymn 4 says Zeus commanded that Hermes be lord over birds of omen, lions, boars, dogs, all flocks and all sheep, and the only appointed messenger to Hades.
hermes-c29 (scholarly-interpretation): According to the Dictionary of Greek and Roman Biography and Mythology reproduced on Theoi, Hermes is said to have invented the alphabet, numbers, astronomy, music, the art of fighting, gymnastics, olive cultivation, measures, weights, and many other things.
hermes-c30 (scholarly-interpretation): According to the dictionary reproduced on Theoi, Hermes had cunning in words and actions, even fraud, perjury, and an inclination to steal, but always committed with skill, dexterity, and gracefulness.
hermes-c31 (scholarly-interpretation): According to the dictionary reproduced on Theoi, Hermes, a son of Zeus and Maia, the daughter of Atlas, was born in a cave of Mount Cyllene in Arcadia, but Philostratus places his birth in Olympus.
hermes-c32 (scholarly-interpretation): According to the dictionary reproduced on Theoi, Apollo gave Hermes his golden shepherd's staff and taught him prophesying by dice, and Zeus made him his own herald and herald of the gods of the lower world.
hermes-c33 (historical-record): According to the dictionary reproduced on Theoi, things sacred to Hermes included the palm tree, the tortoise, the number four, and several kinds of fish, and offerings to him consisted of incense, honey, cakes, pigs, and especially lambs and young goats.
hermes-c34 (etymology): The name Hermes has long been derived from Greek herma ('stone heap, cairn'), but this scholarly speculation is also disputed.
hermes-c35 (historical-record): The earliest form of the name Hermes (Ἑρμῆς) is the Mycenaean Greek form written in the Linear B syllabic script.
hermes-c36 (scholarly-interpretation): According to a theory that has received considerable scholarly acceptance, Hermes originated as a form of the god Pan.
hermes-c37 (historical-record): Perhaps the most celebrated depiction of Hermes in Greek art is the statue by Praxiteles (c. 330 BCE) that once stood in the temple of Hera at Olympia and now resides in the site's archaeological museum.
hermes-c38 (historical-record): In the Hellenistic period the god was often associated with gymnasia and seen as the protector of youths.
hermes-c39 (traditional-belief): In mythology Hermes was also the father of the pastoral god Pan and of Eudoros (with Polymele), a leader of the Myrmidons, although he was not given a wife in any Greek myth.
hermes-c40 (scholarly-interpretation): According to the dictionary reproduced on Theoi, other accounts place the theft of the oxen at a more advanced period of the god's life.
hermes-c41 (scholarly-interpretation): According to the dictionary reproduced on Theoi, Hermes's staff is frequently mentioned in the Homeric poems as the magic staff with which he closes and opens the eyes of mortals.
hermes-c42 (scholarly-interpretation): According to the dictionary reproduced on Theoi, Hermes is also described as the god who had it in his power to send refreshing sleep or to take it away.
hermes-c43 (historical-record): Such tablets have been discovered at Pylos, Thebes, and Knossos.
