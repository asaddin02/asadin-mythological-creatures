
=== persephone (lengkap-informasi, batch-036)
Persephone | tier rich | deity | jenis dewa | greek-mythology
Ringkas: Persephone, also called Kore, is a Greek goddess of agriculture and vegetation, daughter of Demeter, who became queen of the underworld as Hades's wife and returns to the surface each year.
Kemampuan: null — In the Homeric Hymn Hades declares Persephone shall rule all that lives and moves and have the greatest rights among the gods; those who defraud her shall be punished. [persephone-c28]

persephone-c01 (religious-tradition): Persephone is a Greek goddess of agriculture and vegetation and wife of Hades.
persephone-c02 (etymology): Kore means daughter and maiden.
persephone-c03 (religious-tradition): As Hades's wife, Persephone is queen of the underworld.
persephone-c04 (traditional-belief): Hades abducted Persephone while she picked flowers and took her to the underworld.
persephone-c05 (traditional-belief): Demeter's grief caused crop failure, prompting Zeus to demand Persephone's return.
persephone-c06 (traditional-belief): Pomegranate seeds obliged Persephone to spend part of the year below and the rest with Demeter.
persephone-c07 (scholarly-interpretation): The story may symbolize seasonal change and life's cycle.
persephone-c08 (historical-record): Persephone was linked to the Eleusinian Mysteries and Thesmophoria.
persephone-c09 (historical-record): The Eleusinian relief depicts Persephone on the right as Demeter's daughter.
persephone-c10 (religious-tradition): Hades, god of the Underworld, fell in love with the beautiful Persephone when he saw her picking flowers in a meadow, then carried her off in his chariot to the dark Underworld.
persephone-c11 (religious-tradition): In some accounts Zeus had consented to the abduction, and the location is traditionally placed in either Sicily (famed for its fertility) or Asia.
persephone-c12 (religious-tradition): Demeter searched the earth for her lost daughter; though Helios (or Hermes) told her her daughter's fate, she kept wandering until she arrived at Eleusis.
persephone-c13 (religious-tradition): Once the temple was finished, Demeter withdrew into it and created a great drought to convince the other gods to release Persephone; as the drought claimed more victims, Zeus sent Hermes to persuade Hades to release her.
persephone-c14 (religious-tradition): Before giving her up, the wily Hades put a pomegranate kernel in her mouth, knowing its divine taste would compel her to return to him.
persephone-c15 (religious-tradition): As a compromise, it was decided Persephone would be released but must return to Hades for one-third of the year (or one-half in other accounts).
persephone-c16 (scholarly-interpretation): Another interpretation is that the myth represents the Greeks storing their grain underground for part of the year to protect it from summer heat.
persephone-c17 (historical-record): Persephone's cult was especially strong in Sicily and southern Italy; besides the Eleusinian Mysteries at Eleusis there were sanctuaries across the Greek world, notably at Locri Epizephyrii, Mantinea, Megalopolis and Sparta.
persephone-c18 (religious-tradition): The Thesmophoria was a Greek-wide celebration of the goddess and her mother, exclusive to women and held annually before sowing, when sacrifices were made and putrefied pig remains were mixed with the seeds.
persephone-c19 (religious-tradition): As Queen of the Underworld, Persephone was often appealed to in curse tablets and on inscribed gold leaves buried with dead followers of Orphism.
persephone-c20 (religious-tradition): In that guise she was seen as a protectress in the after-life, although Hesiod repeatedly calls her "dread Persephone" in his Theogony.
persephone-c21 (historical-record): Persephone rarely appears in art before the 6th century BCE, then is usually shown with Demeter; both often wear crowns and hold a torch, sceptre or stalks of grain.
persephone-c22 (religious-tradition): In various other myths Persephone is the mother of Dionysos (by Zeus, who is also her father), though Semele is the more usual candidate, and she squabbles with Aphrodite over Adonis, the two agreeing to share the lover in turns.
persephone-c23 (historical-record): A famous relief slab from Eleusis shows Demeter and Persephone (holding a torch) on either side of Triptolemos; it dates to the 5th century BCE.
persephone-c24 (religious-tradition): In other versions Persephone could have been released had she eaten nothing in the underworld, but at the last moment Hades gave her a pomegranate seed.
persephone-c25 (religious-tradition): In the Homeric Hymn to Demeter the girl was amazed and reached out with both hands for the lovely flower; then the earth yawned in the plain of Nysa and the lord, Host of Many, sprang out with his immortal horses.
persephone-c26 (religious-tradition): The narcissus was made to grow by Earth at the will of Zeus and to please the Host of Many, as a snare for the bloom-like girl.
persephone-c27 (religious-tradition): Only tender-hearted Hecate, daughter of Persaeus, heard the girl from her cave, and the lord Helios, Hyperion's bright son.
persephone-c28 (religious-tradition): In the Homeric Hymn Hades tells Persephone that while she is there she shall rule all that lives and moves and have the greatest rights among the gods; those who defraud her and do not appease her power with offerings shall be punished for evermore.
persephone-c29 (religious-tradition): Hades secretly gave her sweet pomegranate seed to eat, taking care that she might not remain continually with Demeter.
persephone-c30 (religious-tradition): Demeter says that if Persephone has tasted food she must go back beneath the earth to dwell a third part of the seasons every year, and for the other two parts be with her and the other gods.
persephone-c31 (religious-tradition): When the earth blooms with the fragrant flowers of spring, Persephone shall come up once more from the realm of darkness to be a wonder for gods and mortals.
persephone-c32 (religious-tradition): From that time Hecate was minister and companion to Persephone.
persephone-c33 (religious-tradition): Persephone (Περσεφόνη in Ancient Greek) is the daughter of Zeus and Demeter; she became queen of the underworld after her abduction by her uncle Hades, king of the underworld, who later took her into marriage.
persephone-c34 (scholarly-interpretation): The myth of her abduction, sojourn in the underworld and cyclical return to the surface represents her functions as embodiment of spring and personification of vegetation, especially grain crops.
persephone-c35 (historical-record): In Classical Greek art Persephone is invariably robed, often carrying a sheaf of grain; she may appear as a mystical divinity with a sceptre and a little box, but was mostly shown being carried off by Hades.
persephone-c36 (religious-tradition): Persephone as a vegetation goddess and her mother Demeter were the central figures of the Eleusinian Mysteries, which promised initiates a happy afterlife.
persephone-c37 (religious-tradition): In the region of the city of Epizephyrian Locris, in modern Calabria (southern Italy), she is a goddess of marriage and childbirth.
persephone-c38 (religious-tradition): In Latin her name is rendered Proserpina; the Romans identified her as Libera, an Italic goddess conflated with Proserpina.
persephone-c39 (etymology): The etymology of "Persephone" is obscure; a popular folk etymology links it to pherein phonon, "to bring (or cause) death".
persephone-c40 (scholarly-interpretation): Günther Zuntz considers "Persephone" and "Kore" distinct deities ("no farmer prayed for corn to Persephone; no mourner thought of the dead as being with Kore"), but ancient Greek writers were not as consistent as he claims.
persephone-c41 (scholarly-interpretation): Myths similar to Persephone's descent and return also appear in the cults of male gods, including Attis, Adonis and Osiris, and in Minoan Crete.
persephone-c42 (scholarly-interpretation): Persephone's epithets reveal her double function as chthonic and vegetation goddess.
persephone-c43 (scholarly-interpretation): According to a hypothesis by Rudolf Wachter, the combined sense of the name would be "she who beats the ears of corn", i.e. a "thresher of grain".
persephone-c44 (religious-tradition): In cult she was often referred to as Kore.
